\chapter{Related Work}
\label{chap:related-work}

\section{Edge LLM inference and single-board-computer benchmarking}
Running a language model without a network connection or a data-centre accelerator is no longer a
niche exercise, and a growing body of work profiles what single-board computers can sustain when
asked to do it. The broadest of these surveys benchmarks twenty-five quantised language
models across three single-board computers under two inference runtimes, and reports a
parameter ceiling --- above roughly 1.5~billion parameters no board in its sample serves a model
reliably --- together with a throughput spread of up to four times that is attributable to the
runtime rather than to the model~\cite{sbc2025}. That result establishes the shape of the problem
this thesis inherits: on CPU-only edge hardware throughput is the scarce resource, the models that
fit within it are small ones, and the largest single lever on it is the inference runtime rather
than the board.

What that survey and its relatives do not report is what happens to a model once it is asked to do
something with the tokens it produces. Their outcome metric is throughput -- tokens per second,
latency, memory footprint -- measured independently of any downstream task, so a configuration that
clears a throughput floor and one that clears it while degenerating into ungrammatical or
semantically wrong output are indistinguishable in their tables. This thesis narrows the scope to
one task, one device, and four candidate models (Qwen2.5-0.5B~\cite{qwen25},
SmolLM2-360M~\cite{smollm2}, Llama-3.2-1B~\cite{llama32}, and H2O-Danube3-500M~\cite{danube3}), and
in exchange measures what the survey literature does not: exact match, intent and slot F1, and
schema validity. Three of those models are carried through to the quantised artefact and reported
against the same throughput floor; the fourth is held at the reference precision as a
parameter-matched control, for the reason Chapter~\ref{chap:method} sets out. The accuracy cost of meeting a
throughput budget on a single-board computer is treated here as a number to be measured, not an
assumption carried over from a benchmark that stops counting at tokens per second.

The runtime that makes this measurement possible on the target hardware is llama.cpp, a C\,++
inference engine for quantised language models with no dependency on a GPU driver or a Python
runtime~\cite{llamacpp}. Its GGUF weight format and its k-quant schemes are what let a
0.5~B-parameter model fit and run inside the memory and thread budget of a Raspberry~Pi~5 at all;
Section~\ref{sec:quantisation} takes up what those schemes cost in accuracy.

\section{Quantisation}
\label{sec:quantisation}
Post-training quantisation is the standard route from a model trained in floating point to one
that fits the memory budget of edge hardware, and llama.cpp's k-quant family is the specific
scheme this thesis deploys under. Q8\_0 stores each weight block at eight bits with a per-block
scale, and Q4\_K\_M packs the same blocks into four bits with a mixed-precision correction term
that spends more bits on the blocks quantisation error is largest in~\cite{llamacpp}. Both cut a
model's weight footprint by roughly half or three-quarters against a Raspberry~Pi~5's memory
budget, which is what makes a Q8\_0 or Q4\_K\_M artefact the object of interest here rather than
the fp16 checkpoint training produces.

The accuracy cost of that reduction is, in the runtime's own documentation and in most reported
uses of it, expressed as a perplexity delta on a generic language-modelling corpus, not as a
change in accuracy on any specific downstream task. A perplexity number averaged over held-out
text says how much less confident the quantised model is about the next token in general; it says
nothing about whether the intent it assigns to a spoken command, or the slot values it fills in,
still match the ground truth once the weights that produced them have been rounded to four or
eight bits. For a task where the model's output is executed by a flight controller rather than
read by a person, that is the gap that matters, and it is routinely left as an assumption --
quantisation is deployed because it is expected to be cheap, not because the specific cost on the
specific task has been measured. Closing that gap for exact match, intent and slot F1, and schema
validity, at both Q8\_0 and Q4\_K\_M and for every fine-tuned candidate model, is Contribution C3;
Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reports what it measured.

\section{Constrained decoding}
\label{sec:constrained-decoding}
Grammar-constrained generation restricts, at every decoding step, the set of tokens the model is
allowed to sample to those consistent with a formal grammar, so that no output can leave the
grammar's language regardless of what the model would otherwise have produced. GBNF (GGML BNF) is
the grammar formalism llama.cpp implements this way: a context-free grammar compiled once and then
used to mask the decoder's token distribution at each step, at a fixed per-token
overhead~\cite{llamacpp}. This is a mechanically different guarantee from prompting a model to
produce well-formed output and then checking the result -- the grammar acts inside the sampling
loop, before an invalid token can ever be emitted, rather than after a full sequence has already
been generated.

That difference in mechanism is also a difference in what kind of claim can be made about
structural validity. Without a grammar, whether a model's output parses against a schema is an
empirical question, answered by counting how often it does so on some test set, and the answer
degrades however the input distribution the model is asked to handle does. With a grammar
compiled from the same schema, the question is closed by construction: every output is
grammar-valid because no other kind of output is reachable, on any input, in or out of the
distribution the model was fine-tuned on. Constrained decoding, in other words, moves structural
validity from a rate that fine-tuning and a favourable test set can inflate to a property that the
decoder enforces independently of both. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reports a grammar ablation
built to make that distinction measurable rather than asserted: the same fine-tuned model, decoded
with and without the grammar, isolates what the grammar itself contributes once fine-tuning has
already done most of the work of keeping output well-formed.

This literature is not revisited in the companion \emph{M\'emoire d'Ing\'enieur}: constrained
decoding and the GBNF grammar it is built on are the concern of this document alone, which owns
the command schema they constrain (Chapter~\ref{chap:method}); the \emph{M\'emoire d'Ing\'enieur}
cites the resulting grammar as a fixed input to the validation layers it builds on top of it,
without re-deriving the argument made here.

\section{Spoken-language understanding for robotics}
\label{sec:slu-robotics}
Parsing an utterance into an intent and a set of slot values is a long-settled task shape in
spoken-language understanding, not one this thesis introduces. MASSIVE, a one-million-example
multilingual natural-language-understanding dataset spanning dozens of languages and dozens of
intents, is built around exactly this shape and demonstrates that models trained at sufficient
scale reach high intent and slot accuracy on it~\cite{massive}. Robot command parsing inherits that
shape directly: a spoken instruction to a robot is, structurally, an utterance to be mapped to one
intent among a fixed set and a handful of slot values, and nothing about that mapping is specific
to robotics.

What is specific to this deployment is not the shape of the task but the resource envelope it has
to be solved inside. MASSIVE-scale results are obtained by models with no constraint on where they
run or how much compute they may use per query, and the human-facing systems built on that shape
typically have a person reading the output before anything acts on it. Neither assumption holds
here: the model has to run offline, at sub-billion-parameter scale, inside the throughput and
memory budget a Raspberry~Pi~5 provides, and its output is consumed directly by a flight
controller with no human in the loop to catch a plausible-looking mistake. Intent and slot parsing
being a solved problem in the abstract says nothing about whether it can be solved at this size,
under this budget, with an error rate low enough to hand the result to a swarm of aircraft
unread -- that is the question this thesis's evaluation protocol
(Chapter~\ref{chap:method}) is built to answer.

\section{Positioning}
\label{sec:positioning}

\paragraph{Synthesis.} Each of the four literatures reviewed in this chapter is, on its own,
mature. Single-board-computer benchmarking has established that throughput is the binding
constraint on this hardware class, that it bounds the servable model to roughly 1.5~billion
parameters, and that the runtime chosen to serve that model moves the bound further than the board
does~\cite{sbc2025}. Quantisation is the accepted technique for fitting a model to that
constraint, with an accuracy cost that is conventionally reported in terms the runtime itself
provides -- a perplexity delta on a generic corpus -- rather than in terms of any specific
downstream task~\cite{llamacpp}. Grammar-constrained decoding offers a mechanism, independent of
either of the first two literatures, for making a model's output structurally reliable by
construction rather than by the accuracy of the model that produced it~\cite{llamacpp}. Intent and
slot parsing, finally, is a settled task shape, demonstrated at scale on corpora built for
exactly it~\cite{massive}. None of these four bodies of work is in tension with any other; they
simply have not been evaluated together.

\paragraph{Gap.} That absence of intersection is the gap. Single-board-computer surveys report
throughput without reference to any task, so they cannot say what a throughput-passing
configuration costs in task accuracy. Quantisation is reported against generic
perplexity, not against exact match, intent and slot F1, or schema validity on a specific
command-parsing task, so its cost on a task a flight controller depends on is an assumption
carried by convention rather than a number anyone has measured. Grammar-constrained decoding is
presented as a mechanism for structural reliability, but nothing in the literature reviewed here
separates what the grammar itself contributes from what a model already fine-tuned to produce
that structure would have done regardless -- the two are conflated whenever a grammar is used only
alongside fine-tuning and never against the same model without it. And intent-and-slot parsing at
MASSIVE scale says nothing about the same task shape solved offline, at sub-billion-parameter
scale, under a hard latency floor, with output consumed directly by a controller rather than read
by a person. Each literature answers its own question and is silent on this one.

\paragraph{Delta.} The three contributions of this thesis close that gap directly, one literature
each. Contribution C1 -- the frozen command schema, its matched GBNF grammar, and the two-layer
semantic validator built on top of it -- isolates the grammar's own contribution to structural
validity by measuring the same fine-tuned model with and without it, rather than presenting the
combination as a single undifferentiated design. Contribution C2 -- the label-first dataset built
and sealed for this vocabulary -- supplies the task-specific data that a general-purpose
intent-and-slot corpus such as MASSIVE does not, and that this task-under-this-budget question
requires. Contribution C3 -- the quantised-inference harness that reports exact match, intent and
slot F1, schema validity, and false-command rate at each quantisation level, on the deployed
Raspberry~Pi~5, for every fine-tuned candidate model -- turns the accuracy cost of quantisation
from an assumption into a measured quantity, on the exact task and the exact hardware this thesis
targets. Chapters~\ref{chap:method} and~\ref{chap:results} report how each was built and what it
measured.
