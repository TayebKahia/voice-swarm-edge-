\chapter{State of the art}
\label{chap:state-of-the-art}
This chapter places the system of Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} among the published work it
resembles. Section~\ref{sec:voice-uav} reviews systems that turn spoken or written language into
the motion of aerial and other robots, organised by the properties the requirements of
Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements} make decisive. Section~\ref{sec:lim-positioning}
positions this work against the drone-control agent of Lim et al.~\cite{lim2025} and states why
the two are compared by design rather than by measurement. Sections~\ref{sec:swarm-control-sota}
and~\ref{sec:speech-components} cover the swarm-control model the controller builds on and the
offline speech components the runtime deploys, the latter justified against this system's own
requirements. Section~\ref{sec:gap} states the gap this document addresses and concedes the parts
of it that published systems already cover. The literature on edge inference, quantisation,
constrained decoding and spoken-language understanding is reviewed in the \emph{M\'emoire de
Master} and is not repeated here.

\section{Voice-controlled UAV systems}
\label{sec:voice-uav}
Language interfaces to \glspl{uav} have moved from recognisers matched against a fixed command
language to \glspl{llm} that plan, generate programs or call flight functions, and a
recent survey maps that field and the directions it is taking~\cite{javaid2024}. Four properties of
each published system decide how close it lies to the system of this document: whether its input is
speech or typed text, whether it commands one vehicle or a group, where its language model runs,
and what check, if any, stands between the model's output and the vehicle. The requirements of
Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} fix all four: speech, five vehicles, a model on the device, and
three automated checks. Table~\ref{tab:voice-uav-survey} records the four properties for the
systems reviewed in this section.

\paragraph{Fixed command languages.} The earlier generation of voice control needed no language
model, because it confined the operator to a command language fixed in advance. Landau and van
Delden map the output of the Nuance speech-recognition platform onto flight commands for a DJI
Phantom~4 through regular expressions that define the control language~\cite{landau2017}.
Contreras et al.\ improve a cloud-based recogniser for \gls{uav} control by matching its output
against the phonemes of a domain vocabulary, which raises English command recognition from 74.81\%
to 93.33\% in a simulated domestic scene, and they evaluate it with several levels of distortion
applied to the spoken commands~\cite{contreras2020}. Constraining what the operator may say is
therefore an established practice in this field, not one this document introduces. The second study
is also a precedent for measuring recognition as the acoustic conditions degrade, which the
acoustic-robustness experiment of Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} does across a range of \gls{snr}.
Both systems command a single vehicle.

\paragraph{Language-model control.} \Glspl{llm} replaced the fixed command language
with generated plans, programs or function calls, and the check between the model's output and
actuation became the central design question. SayCan constrains a language model to the actions a
robot can perform by weighting its proposals with the learned value functions of pretrained
skills~\cite{saycan2022}. Code as Policies generates programs that compose given perception and
control primitives, and its authors state that the approach assumes every given instruction is
feasible and cannot tell in advance whether a response will be correct~\cite{codeaspolicies2023}.
Vemprala et al.\ apply ChatGPT, through prompt design and a library of robot functions, to tasks
that include aerial navigation; the check there is a human on the loop, who evaluates the output for
quality and safety before it is executed, and the authors advise that such tools should not be given
full control of the robotics pipeline~\cite{chatgptrobotics2024}. A learned feasibility score, a
primitive library whose correctness is assumed, and human review are the three positions against
which the validation layers of Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} are set. Those layers are automated
and deterministic, and their rejection is a hold (Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture}).

\paragraph{Single-vehicle drone agents.} Systems built specifically for drones make the latency of
generation explicit. TypeFly observes that sequential token generation introduces a latency that
grows with the length of the plan, and it answers by changing the output representation: a
token-efficient plan language, interpreted while it streams, reduces response time by up to 62\%
and keeps it below 1.5~s on a benchmark of eleven tasks, with GPT-4 as a remote model and a typed
task description as input~\cite{typefly2024}. That answer shortens the path every command takes.
The answer of this document to the same pressure is architectural: the stopping command is removed
from the language-model path altogether (Section~\ref{sec:dual-path}). Chat with UAV splits
planning from execution between two language-model agents~\cite{chatwithuav2025}. It is a dual
architecture but not a dual path in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:dual-path}, since both of its
branches run at the latency of a language model. Phadke et al.\ couple existing language models to
robotic simulators through a modular template framework~\cite{phadke2024}. Silva and Burke report
what they describe as the broadest evaluation of a language-model command interface for drones on
the axes it measures: more than 1{,}000 simulated flights, 110 geofence-violation trials in which
no aircraft left the permitted zone, and three real quadcopters, with the language model treated as
an untrusted commander whose output is validated on the server~\cite{silva2026}. Validation
downstream of a model that is not trusted is the arrangement this document adopts; the models that
evaluation uses are mostly those of cloud providers.

\paragraph{Pipeline shape and latency.} Two studies measure the latency cost of the pipeline's
shape directly, and both bear on the reflex path. Sim\~oes et al.\ compare, for one Tello drone
commanded in Portuguese, a cascade of speech recognition and a language model against a classifier
that maps speech directly to a command: the cascade reaches an accuracy of 0.81 at 1.233~s, the
direct classifier 0.99 at 0.021~s~\cite{simoes2024}. Henry et al.\ report, on the explicit simple
commands of a French corpus of spontaneous human--drone interaction, 93\% at 7~ms for a
transcription-free intent recogniser against 79\% at 202~ms for a cascade of Whisper and
CamemBERT-Large, and they attribute that 14-point gap to the cascade architecture rather than to
recognition quality. On the full spontaneous test set the figures are 82\% and 59\%, and the 7~ms
is measured on a \gls{gpu} (106~ms on a \gls{cpu})~\cite{henry2026}. The asymmetry between the reflex
budget and the parse budget of Table~\ref{tab:nonfunctional-requirements} is therefore a property of
pipeline shape documented independently of this work, and not an artefact of this device. Both
studies compare the two shapes as alternative pipelines and select between them. In this document
they run side by side, and the question that follows --- which commands the direct path may carry
--- does not arise in a comparison of alternatives. Section~\ref{sec:membership-rule} answers it.

\paragraph{Where the model runs.} The offline requirement of Section~\ref{sec:operational-context}
fixes where the language model runs, and the literature records what that costs. Sikorski et al.\
command a wheeled mobile robot by speech, recognised offline with VOSK, and compare GPT-4-Turbo in
the cloud against LLaMA~2-7B quantised to Q5\_K\_M and run offline on a control computer; the
offline model exhibits significant limitations in consistency and reliability~\cite{sikorski2025}.
That result states the cost this design absorbs rather than denies, and the \emph{M\'emoire de
Master} measures it for the models that can run on this device. Torkamani and Zarin route spoken
commands for IoT devices between edge and cloud according to processor load, device temperature and
network latency, with the edge tier on an NVIDIA Jetson platform~\cite{torkamani2025}. Routing of
that kind presumes that the network is available at least part of the time, which the deployment
site of Section~\ref{sec:operational-context} does not assure. Offline operation is a requirement
here, not a routing policy.

\paragraph{Swarms.} Language interfaces to swarms are more recent. Strobel et al.\ distinguish
indirect integration, in which a language model synthesises and validates controllers before or
during deployment, from direct integration, in which a separate language-model instance runs on
each robot, and they describe their contribution as mainly conceptual~\cite{llm2swarm2024}.
SwarmChat offers voice, text and teleoperation interfaces to a robotic swarm over ROS~2 through
modules described as based on language models; in its implementation, intent recognition is
rule-based keyword matching, which its authors list as a limitation, and its evaluation is
preliminary~\cite{swarmchat2025}. Iannoli et al.\ evaluate six general-purpose language models on
four swarm missions from natural-language text, in ArduPilot software-in-the-loop simulation. They
find that the models still struggle to execute even simple swarm tasks reliably without explicit
grounding and execution support, and that task-specific planning tools and runtime guardrails
substantially improve robustness~\cite{iannoli2026}. That finding is independent support for placing
validation downstream of the model rather than relying on the model. SkySim~\cite{skysim2026} and
CommandSwarm~\cite{commandswarm2026} are the two swarm systems nearest to this one, and
Section~\ref{sec:gap} examines them property by property.

\section{Positioning against Lim et al.}
\label{sec:lim-positioning}
The agent of Lim et al.~\cite{lim2025} is the baseline for this document because it is the published
system nearest to this one in the part of the design the \emph{M\'emoire de Master} fixes: open
language models of the same families, Qwen2.5 and Llama-3.2 among them, hosted locally rather than by
a cloud provider, turning natural language into commands for a PX4-based drone. The agent prompts
Gemma3, Qwen2.5, Llama-3.2 and DeepSeek-LLM zero-shot through Ollama, served from an RTX~3080~Ti
workstation on the local network, with no fine-tuning. Output is constrained by a sampling
temperature of 0.2 and by a prompt that fixes the format of turn and move commands. The mission
starts from a text message on ROS~2, and a single quadcopter is flown to a goal, with a
vision-language model detecting the target. Three of the four models produce valid flight commands
in all cases and DeepSeek-LLM in 38\%, the invalid outputs consisting of extraneous text, symbols
and markdown; the highest task success, 40\%, is obtained with Gemma3 and the vision-language model.
The fallback is a human operator who switches the vehicle to position mode, in which it hovers.
Latency is not reported.

Table~\ref{tab:lim-positioning} sets the two systems side by side, and they differ on every axis
that determines latency or accuracy. Without speech input, the figures of Lim et al.\ contain no
recognition error and no endpointing wait, so neither the acoustic-robustness experiment nor the
parse-path latency of this document has a counterpart there. A valid-command rate under zero-shot
prompting measures how reliably a model follows a format it has been asked to follow; under a
decoding grammar, validity is a property of the grammar, and the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}, which
owns that mechanism, reports it for the deployed parser. Any latency, had one been reported, would
be dominated by the difference between a desktop \gls{gpu} and four \gls{cpu} cores. Task success is
defined over goal-directed flight with visual detection, a task this system does not perform, while
formation accuracy is defined over a task Lim et al.\ do not attempt. Finally, a stop that waits for
a human operator offers nothing against which the reflex latency of this system could be compared.

\section{Swarm control}
\label{sec:swarm-control-sota}
Reynolds' model of flocking produces coordinated group motion from three rules applied by each
individual to its nearby flockmates: avoid collisions with them, match their velocity, and stay
close to them~\cite{reynolds1987}. The model needs no leader and no global plan, and the group terms
of this system's controller derive from it. The controller of Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}
departs from it in three places, each for a reason specific to this system. The command is a
formation rather than an emergent flock: an utterance names a shape, and slot assignment with a
\gls{pid} law on each vehicle's error to its slot determines the trajectory near the formation,
while the flocking terms keep the group together on the approach and are weighted low
(Section~\ref{sec:swarm-controller}). The centring and velocity-matching rules act over the whole
swarm rather than over each vehicle's neighbours, the central-force form that Reynolds' local model
avoids, which is acceptable for five vehicles organised by slots. Collision avoidance is taken out
of the flocking rules and given to an artificial potential field, whose failure modes
Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} identifies~\cite{koren1991}, backed by a geometric separation clamp
at the integrator.

In the terms of Strobel et al.~\cite{llm2swarm2024}, this design is neither indirect nor direct
integration: the language model neither synthesises the controller nor runs on each vehicle. A
single model instance, on the operator's device, maps an utterance to a formation-level command for a
conventional controller, and the controller's behaviour between commands involves no language model.
This separation carries the safety argument of Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem}. The stage that can
be wrong in the four ways listed there is confined to choosing the command, and separation between
vehicles is enforced by a mechanism that does not consult it. SkySim arrives at the same layering
independently: a cloud-hosted planner sits above an artificial-potential-field layer running at
20~Hz that repels vehicles from one another, with speed saturated at 0.5~m/s, for swarms of 3, 10
and 30 simulated drones~\cite{skysim2026}. The layering is therefore not claimed as new
(Section~\ref{sec:gap}).

The nearest systems are simulation-based: SkySim evaluates in Gazebo with ROS~2, and CommandSwarm
targets a PyGame-based swarm simulator but scores its generated trees without executing
them~\cite{skysim2026,commandswarm2026}. This work also evaluates in simulation. PyFlyt
provides \gls{uav} simulation environments for reinforcement-learning research on the Bullet
physics engine~\cite{pyflyt}, with rigid-body and rotor dynamics and its own attitude control. It
is one of two backends behind the controller's environment interface, the other being a kinematic
simulator, and the same controller with the same gains runs on both
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation-backends}). The physics backend tests whether the controller carries
across to rigid-body dynamics; the formation-control experiment runs on the kinematic backend, and
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what that leaves unestablished.

\section{Offline speech components}
\label{sec:speech-components}
Three speech components run on the device --- a keyword spotter, a \gls{vad} model and an
\gls{stt} model --- and a fourth, a \gls{tts} system, is used only to build the keyword spotter's
training corpus. No comparative study was found that selects among open components of these kinds
on a four-core single-board processor under a latency budget, and for two of them, openWakeWord and
Silero~\gls{vad}, no peer-reviewed description was found at all; both are software releases and are cited
as such. Each choice is therefore justified by the requirements of this system rather than by a
published comparison. There are four such requirements. The component runs with no network access
(Table~\ref{tab:functional-requirements}). It fits its row of the latency budget
(Table~\ref{tab:stage-budget}) on the cores allocated to it (Table~\ref{tab:core-allocation}). It
leaves room within the device's 8~GB for the language model; the full deployed stack was not
measured against its memory ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:resource-allocation}). And its licence permits
deployment. The latency evidence for each component is measured on this device, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} reports it.

\paragraph{Keyword spotter.} The reflex budget of 150~ms rests on the keyword spotter. Keyword
spotting is a task distinct from full recognition, with its own datasets and evaluation
methodology~\cite{speechcmd}: its output is a detection rather than a transcript, and its errors are
false accepts and false rejects at an operating point rather than word errors. openWakeWord~\cite{oww}
supplies a frozen feature front end that turns audio into one speech embedding per 80~ms frame, over
which small classifier heads can be trained for arbitrary phrases. That property decided the choice.
The phrases \texttt{swarm hold} and \texttt{swarm abort} occur in no public corpus used here, so the
heads had to be trained on synthetic positives, and heads that are small networks over a fixed
embedding can be trained that way without training an acoustic model. The two heads are fitted by
this work rather than taken from openWakeWord's pre-trained models (Section~\ref{sec:audio-chain}),
and the spotter runs on core~0 beside the endpointer, which leaves cores~1--3 to the parse path.
Section~\ref{sec:keyword-spotter-evaluation} reports the operating point and
Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment} the reflex latency, idle and under load.

\paragraph{Endpointer.} The endpointer decides when an utterance has ended, and the parse path's
clock starts at that decision, so its cost appears twice: as computation on the core it shares with
the spotter, and as the silence it must wait for before it declares the end of speech. Silero
\gls{vad}~\cite{silero} is a pre-trained neural voice-activity detector distributed as an ONNX model that
processes 32~ms windows with a recurrent state. It runs here on ONNX Runtime directly, on one thread
and without the PyTorch dependency of its own wrapper (Section~\ref{sec:audio-chain}). The wait is
set by the silence threshold derived in Section~\ref{sec:latency-budget}, not by the model. What the
model has to supply is a speech probability for each window, computed cheaply enough that the
spotter and the endpointer together complete each 80~ms frame within 80~ms on core~0; the latency
experiment measures that margin (Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment}).

\paragraph{Speech recognition.} Speech recognition is the largest row of the parse-path budget,
1{,}200~ms at p95 on three threads (Table~\ref{tab:stage-budget}). Whisper is trained on 680{,}000
hours of weakly supervised audio, and its smallest English-only model, \texttt{tiny.en}, has 39
million parameters~\cite{whisper}. \texttt{whisper.cpp}~\cite{whispercpp} runs that model in C and
C++ on the \gls{cpu}, with no Python runtime on the inference path. The smallest model was chosen
because recognition and decoding run in sequence on the same three cores, so every millisecond the
recogniser spends is a millisecond the end-to-end budget of 2{,}500~ms cannot give to the language
model. The accuracy cost of the smallest model is the risk this choice carries, and it is measured
rather than assumed: the \emph{M\'emoire de Master} reports the \gls{wer} of \texttt{tiny.en} on the
author's speech against a multi-speaker distribution, and the acoustic-robustness experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:acoustic-robustness}) measures command recognition from the recogniser's
transcripts as noise increases. The recogniser's invocation, including an initial prompt that lists
the command vocabulary, is shared with the dataset pipeline, so the parser is trained on the kind of
transcription errors it receives at run time (Section~\ref{sec:runtime}).

\paragraph{Speech synthesis.} Piper~\cite{piper}, a local neural \gls{tts} system, is not part of
the runtime, and the system produces no spoken output. Piper renders, in three voices, the
synthetic positives on which the keyword spotter's heads are trained, within a corpus that the
\emph{M\'emoire de Master} constructs (Section~\ref{sec:audio-chain}).

\paragraph{Licences.} The licences declared by the deployed packages are permissive: MIT for
Silero~\gls{vad}, \texttt{whisper.cpp} and ONNX Runtime, and Apache~2.0 for openWakeWord. Piper is
distributed under the GPL, version~3 or later; it is used only to generate training data and is not
part of the deployed runtime. The language model on the device, and the runtime that serves it
under a decoding grammar~\cite{llamacpp}, are selected in the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}, which also
reviews the literature on them.

\section{Gap}
\label{sec:gap}
The survey supports a gap stated as a conjunction of properties rather than as any single one. Four
properties together describe the system of this document:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item spoken commands to a group of vehicles, with the language model's output checked
        deterministically before it takes effect;
  \item a planner separated from a lower layer that enforces separation between vehicles at the
        control rate;
  \item a stopping path that preempts language-model inference, under a membership rule derived
        from the cost of a false accept and the value of the time saved;
  \item the whole perception and language chain executing offline on one \gls{sbc},
        with a language model below a billion parameters.
\end{enumerate}
The first two are each covered by a published system, and they are conceded here by name.

\paragraph{CommandSwarm.} CommandSwarm~\cite{commandswarm2026} is the published system nearest to
this one, and it covers the first property. It turns speech or text into behaviour trees for a
robotic swarm with open models of 6.7 to 14 billion parameters, all quantised to 4 bits. Execution
is gated by a deterministic parser that accepts only a whitelist of executable swarm primitives,
behind constrained prompting --- a prompt that lists the permitted actions --- and a safety
classifier. Its authors conclude that parser acceptance and safety filtering remain necessary
execution gates and that generation quality alone is not sufficient, which is the position of
Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} reached independently. Its limits against the other three
properties are recorded as the paper states them. It does not claim offline operation and names no
deployment hardware. Its chosen speech front end, SeamlessM4T v2-large, takes about 4.0~s per
utterance, above the end-to-end budget this system is held to
(Table~\ref{tab:nonfunctional-requirements}), although on unstated hardware; Whisper-medium speech
translation, at about 5.2~s, was the rejected alternative. The generated trees are scored by
text-similarity metrics and parser acceptance rather than executed, the safety classifier is not
independently evaluated, and emergency stop mechanisms appear only among the safeguards that the
authors say a real deployment should require. CommandSwarm validates a completed generation, whereas
a decoding grammar acts during generation; the \emph{M\'emoire de Master} develops that distinction
for its own grammar, and it is not argued here.

\paragraph{SkySim.} SkySim~\cite{skysim2026} covers the second property. Gemini 3.5 Pro, a
cloud-hosted model, plans waypoints from natural-language text, and the artificial-potential-field layer of
Section~\ref{sec:swarm-control-sota} runs beneath it at 20~Hz. Geofencing is performed by the
planner, which rejects waypoints outside the permitted zone and falls back to holding position. That
fallback is close to the rule of Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} that a rejection becomes a hold,
and it is conceded as such. A malformed output or a timeout of the model's interface instead
keeps the waypoints of the previous command rather than issuing a new hold; the paper does not
state what a swarm still converging on those waypoints does. SkySim has no speech input, and its
mean planning latency rises from 34~s for 3 drones to 50~s for 10, with outliers above 100~s, a
latency its authors judge unsuitable for time-critical missions. The separation of a planner from a
lower safety layer is therefore not new, and Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction} does not claim it: the
contribution of Section~\ref{sec:contributions} is the membership rule of the fast path, not the
layering.

\paragraph{Dual-path prior art.} The third property has prior art in its topology, which is cited
here as such. RelayS2S pairs a fast duplex speech-to-speech model, which drafts the opening of a
response, with a slower cascade of recognition and a language model, and a lightweight learned
verifier decides whether the fast draft is committed; the 90th-percentile latency of the first
chunk falls from 1{,}006~ms to 81~ms~\cite{relays2s}. Sahai et al.\ use wake-word detection to
select, frame by frame, which branch of the attention networks inside one recognition model is
executed, the wake-word frames receiving the cheaper branch, in order to save
computation~\cite{dualattn2023}. In both, the fast path serves responsiveness or efficiency rather
than safety, and in the second it is a branch within one model rather than a second path. MIRA is
closer~\cite{mira}. It combines low-latency barge-in preemption and streaming response generation
with deliberative turn decisions in a dual-timescale interaction policy, bounds the robot's physical
commitment to a short, interruptible prefix, and enforces physical safety constraints in a
robot-side layer at the control rate. Its fast interruption gate works from voice activity alone,
without transcription: when the user speaks during the robot's playback, it dispatches an abort,
stops the speech output and ends the robot's motion, after which the robot holds its last safe pose.
An uninterpreted stop followed by a hold, preemption of the slower path, and a control-rate safety
layer corresponding to the separation clamp at the integrator of this system are all present in
MIRA, and none of them is claimed here.

\paragraph{The membership rule.} What this document adds to MIRA concerns what the fast path
chooses. In MIRA, the fast path reacts to the presence of user speech during the robot's own
playback and has one action, the abort of that response; what the user said is decided afterwards
by the deliberative path. The reflex path here discriminates between phrases, at any time, and
issues one of two distinct swarm commands, a hold or an abort, to five vehicles without
transcription or parsing, the two stages that establish what the operator said
(Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem}). A path that chooses between commands without interpreting the
utterance raises a question that a path with a single action does not, namely which commands it may
issue. The membership rule of Section~\ref{sec:membership-rule} answers that question from the
cost of a false accept and the value of the time saved, and the path is held to a latency budget of
its own that must be met while the language model decodes. The rule and that budget are the
contribution of Section~\ref{sec:contributions}. MIRA also addresses dialogue with a single embodied
companion rather than a swarm, and it does not name its recognition and language models or say
where they run, so offline operation is not addressed.

\paragraph{Single-board operation.} The fourth property is the one most plainly open among the sources
reviewed. No system in Table~\ref{tab:voice-uav-survey} executes its full chain on a single-board
computer without a \gls{gpu}. The language models of the systems nearest to this one are hosted by
cloud providers, run on workstation \glspl{gpu}, or run on unstated hardware at 6.7 billion
parameters and above; the one edge device in the table, the Jetson of Torkamani and Zarin, hands
work to the cloud when its runtime metrics call for it. Running the chain on one board is
engineering rather than a research contribution (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}). It belongs to
the gap because it is the condition under which the third property must hold: the reflex budget is
met, or missed, on the same four cores on which the language model decodes
(Section~\ref{sec:resource-allocation}).

The gap this document fills is therefore narrower than any one of the four properties suggests, and
it is stated in that form. Among the systems reviewed, none combines deterministic checking of
spoken swarm commands, a separate control-rate safety layer, a stopping path that preempts the
language model under a derived membership rule, and offline execution on an \gls{sbc} at
sub-billion scale. The first two properties are conceded to CommandSwarm and SkySim. The third is
claimed against RelayS2S, the dual-attention transducer and MIRA, and the fourth is the setting that
turns the third into a problem of processor allocation. Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} designs the
system that fills this gap.
