\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

\section{Operational context}
\label{sec:operational-context}
The system described in this document places one operator in front of five aircraft and gives
that operator a single control surface: speech. The formation is addressed as a unit --- an
utterance such as ``form a circle with radius five metres'' commands five vehicles at once, where
a manual interface would require either five sets of stick inputs or a ground-control station the
operator has to look down at. The unit of command is therefore the formation rather than the
vehicle, and the operator's attention stays on the airspace instead of on a screen. That is the
first constraint the system inherits, and it is an interface constraint before it is a modelling
one.

The second constraint comes from where the operator stands. The deployment setting is an outdoor
site chosen for the flight rather than for its connectivity: no operator-controlled access point,
no assurance of cellular coverage, and no prepared infrastructure of any kind. A pipeline stage
that reaches the network is consequently a stage whose availability is a property of the site and
not of the system, and no requirement can be placed on it that the site is able to violate.

Offline operation is therefore a requirement and not a preference, for three reasons of which any
one alone would be sufficient. Availability is the first, as above. Latency is the second: a
remote call inserts a tail the system does not control into a loop that must hold a p95 budget,
and a budget is defensible only when the worst case of every stage in it is locally bounded
(Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}). The safety path is the third and the most binding.
The two spoken phrases that stop the swarm have to remain available precisely when other parts of
the deployment have failed, and a command channel that can be partitioned away from the aircraft
is not a fail-safe in any useful sense. Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements} states this as
NFR-14, verified by an end-to-end run with networking disabled.

What that decision puts on the device is the entire chain. The keyword spotter, the voice-activity
detector, the speech-recognition model, the language model, the validator and the swarm controller
all execute on one Raspberry Pi~5 with 8~GB of memory, sharing four Cortex-A76 cores with each
other and with the operating system. The consequence of that for model selection --- which small language
models can meet a throughput floor on this device at all, and at what accuracy --- is the subject
of the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}, which owns that comparison; the present document takes the
configuration that comparison selects as given and is concerned with the runtime built around it.

One boundary of the operational context must be stated at the outset, because every claim in this
document is scoped by it: the aircraft are simulated. The controller drives a networked
software-in-the-loop simulation of $N = 5$ vehicles at 50~Hz, through a single environment
interface with two interchangeable backends~\cite{pyflyt}. The audio path is not simulated --- the
evaluation corpus is recorded through the deployed microphone in a real room, and the
demonstration runs from a live microphone --- but the flight is. Results reported here therefore
characterise the command pipeline and the controller, not airframe behaviour in wind, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:demonstration} returns to this as a limitation rather than leaving it implicit.

\section{Engineering requirements}
\label{sec:engineering-requirements}

principle that a requirement naming no way to check it is a statement of intent rather than a
requirement. Twelve functional requirements and twenty-one non-functional rows govern the project
as a whole, and they are shared between this document and the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}. The
subset stated here is not a matter of editorial judgement: this document answers RQ2 and RQ3
(Section~\ref{sec:objectives}), so it carries the requirements those two questions resolve to, and
the remainder are stated, verified and discussed by the document that owns RQ1. Every target below
is a commitment made before measurement; the measured values appear in
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}, and no requirement is quoted here with a result attached to it.

Table~\ref{tab:nonfunctional-requirements} contains one ratio that determines the architecture
before any component is chosen. The reflex budget and the parse budget are not one budget with two
tolerances: NFR-1 allows 150~ms and NFR-2 allows 2{,}500~ms, a separation of more than sixteenfold,
and no single path can satisfy both. The two are not even anchored at the same instant: NFR-1 is
measured from the keyword offset, part-way through the utterance, while NFR-2 starts at
end-of-speech. The reflex has to answer while the operator is still speaking, and the parse path
cannot begin until the operator has stopped --- so no amount of tuning brings one path inside both
budgets. NFR-1 also carries a condition that makes it an architectural
requirement rather than a benchmark: it must hold \emph{while} Branch~B is decoding, which is a
statement about scheduling and core allocation on a four-core device, not about the spotter in
isolation. NFR-17 closes the same loop from the other end by bounding the time before the parse
path can accept a new utterance, so that a reflex does not leave the system deaf.

The keyword rows must be read as a pair. NFR-15 and NFR-16 are not two independent claims but a
single operating point selected from one receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curve, and a
detection threshold trades false accepts against false rejects directly: a system quoting only its
false-accept rate has not stated its operating point at all. Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}
therefore reports the full per-class curve with the chosen threshold marked, and this document
quotes the two numbers together wherever either appears.

NFR-12 is stated in the form it will keep for the remainder of this document. The commitment is
zero collisions \emph{observed} across all trials, backed by a hard geometric separation clamp at
the integrator --- not zero collisions guaranteed by the potential field. The distinction is
not modesty but accuracy about what the mechanism provides, and
Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} gives the reason in full. NFR-13 pairs with it: separation
without formation quality would be satisfied by drones that simply keep away from one another.

The requirements not tabulated here fall into two groups. Those belonging to RQ1 --- the command
schema and grammar, the dataset pipeline, the fine-tuning and export procedure, the canonicaliser,
and the accuracy, memory, thermal and false-command targets measured against them --- are stated
and verified in the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}, which owns the model comparison this runtime
consumes the result of. Two further requirements are project-wide verification hygiene rather than
architecture: one CSV row per benchmark trial, from which every table and figure in both documents
derives, and full reproducibility through pinned dependency versions, fixed seeds and a
single-command rerun. Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} reports against both. One requirement sits
across the boundary and is worth naming explicitly: the safe-failure rate, which asks what
proportion of errors resolve to \texttt{unknown} or a hold rather than to a wrong action, is the
numeric form of the design principle Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} argues for. It is measured
in both documents' experiments, and Chapter~\ref{chap:demonstration} returns to what the
measurement showed.

\section{The safety problem}
\label{sec:safety-problem}
In a system that turns speech into vehicle motion, the two ways of being wrong are not equally
expensive. A command that fails to be acted on costs the operator a repetition. A command that is
acted on wrongly moves five aircraft, and it does so with the same authority as a correct one,
because nothing downstream of the parser can tell the difference. The failure surface is wide
enough to make this more than a remote concern: the speech-recognition stage can mishear a word,
the parser can assign the wrong intent to a correctly transcribed sentence, a slot value can be
recovered with the wrong magnitude or sign, and a command correct in isolation can arrive while
the swarm is in a state that cannot accept it. The design objective that follows is not the
minimisation of the error rate, which is the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}'s concern, but control
over the \emph{direction} of the errors that remain. A misheard command must not become motion.

An accuracy target on its own does not express that objective, because an accuracy figure is
silent about what the failures do. Two systems scoring identically on exact match can differ
completely in consequence, one resolving an ambiguous utterance to a hold and the other to a
heading change, and the score that ranks them equal is measuring the wrong property for a
flight-control path. The architecture therefore places three layers between a spoken utterance and
the vehicle: a grammar that constrains the decoder so that structurally invalid output cannot be
emitted, a semantic validator that enforces the meaning constraints a context-free grammar cannot
express, and a flight state machine that admits a command only if the swarm's current state can
accept it. The first layer is specified in the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}, which owns the schema
and grammar; the second and third belong to this document and are designed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture}. What makes them a safety mechanism rather than a filter is the
exit they share: every rejection at every layer resolves to a hold with a log entry, never to a
substituted or best-guess movement. The vocabulary of failure has one word in it.

Those three layers protect against commands that are wrong. They do nothing about commands that
are late, and the distinction matters most for the one command that exists to prevent an accident
already in progress. An operator who sees the formation begin to do something unintended and says
\emph{swarm hold} is making a request whose value decays with every millisecond it spends in a
queue. Routed through the ordinary path, that request inherits the ordinary path's cost: it waits
for the utterance to end, then for transcription, then for a language model to decode a structured
command, then for three validation layers to admit it --- a budget of 2{,}500~ms at p95
(Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}), and a budget whose clock does not start until the
operator has stopped speaking. A stopping command routed through the same path as a movement
command is a stopping command with a movement command's latency, which is the wrong design for the
only utterance in the vocabulary whose purpose is to arrest motion.

The response is to give that utterance its own path. A two-class keyword spotter listens
continuously and reacts to two phrases, \texttt{swarm hold} and \texttt{swarm abort}, without
transcription and without invoking the language model at all, preempting an in-flight decode if
one is running. The reflex is bounded at 150~ms at p95 and is required to hold that bound
\emph{while} the language model is decoding, which is what makes the dual path an argument about
processor allocation rather than a second copy of the same pipeline. The design is contribution
C4 (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}), and Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} derives it in full.

The reflex path introduces a hazard of its own, and the membership rule that governs it is the
part of this design that most repays explanation. A phrase admitted to Branch~A is a phrase that
reaches the swarm without passing through any of the three validation layers; speed is bought by
removing exactly the machinery the previous paragraphs justified. A path that trades validation
for latency may therefore carry only commands whose incorrect execution is harmless, and in a
flight vocabulary the commands meeting that condition are precisely those that remove energy from
the system. A spurious \texttt{swarm hold} costs an unnecessary hover, recoverable by speaking
again. A movement verb admitted to the same path --- a phrase as ordinary as \emph{swarm
forward} --- would on a false accept produce unvalidated motion from a stage with no semantic
check behind it, which is the accident the architecture was built to prevent, arriving by the
route built to prevent it. The membership rule is consequently derived from the latency argument
rather than chosen by preference, it admits exactly two classes, and it is closed: no movement
verb may be added later without invalidating the reason Branch~A is permitted to skip validation.

Command-level correctness is still not the whole of the safety problem, because a stream of
correct commands can produce an unsafe trajectory. Five aircraft converging on assigned formation
slots have crossing paths by construction, and separation between them is maintained by an
artificial potential field, which is a soft mechanism: it shapes the velocity field so that
vehicles repel one another, but with discrete timesteps and bounded actuation a vehicle can still
cross a boundary within a single integration step, and potential fields carry well-documented
failure modes including local minima and oscillation. The architecture therefore adds a hard
geometric separation clamp at the integrator --- at the point where position is actually updated,
rather than where motion is requested. The placement mirrors the flight state machine one level
up: at the command level the last word belongs to a check immediately before the command takes
effect, and at the trajectory level to a check immediately before the position does. Neither
mechanism trusts the stage that produced its input.

That placement determines what this document may claim, and the claim is stated once here in the
form it keeps throughout. The commitment is zero inter-drone collisions \emph{observed} across all
trials, backed by a hard geometric separation clamp at the integrator. It is not a claim that
collisions are impossible, and it is not a claim that the potential field guarantees separation.
The difference between those sentences is the difference between a statement about the trials
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} reports and a statement about every state the controller could ever
reach; the first is what an experiment can establish, and the second would require a proof over
the reachable set that this work does not attempt. The weaker sentence is also the more
informative one, because it names the mechanism the result rests on instead of attributing the
result to a method that cannot supply it. Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} accordingly reports beside
the collision count the number of occasions on which the clamp had to intervene, that being the
count of timesteps at which the potential field alone was not sufficient --- and the reason the
observed figure is evidence of a working mechanism rather than evidence that the mechanism was
never needed.

The safety problem, stated once and carried through the rest of this document, is therefore
threefold: a wrong command must be hard to act on, a stopping command must be fast to act on, and
the execution of any command must be bounded at the point where it takes effect.
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} builds the three mechanisms this requires and
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} realises them; Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} measures whether
each holds.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
The project poses three research questions, of which this document pursues two. Both concern the
system as an operating whole rather than any component within it, and both are stated here in full
because the chapters that follow are organised to answer them. The pipeline they refer to is the
chain named in Section~\ref{sec:operational-context}: a keyword spotter, a voice-activity detector
(VAD), a speech-to-text stage (STT), a grammar-constrained small language model (SLM), the
validator, and the swarm controller.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ2 (Pipeline latency).} Can a fully offline pipeline (keyword spotter + VAD + STT +
grammar-constrained SLM + validator + controller) meet a p95 budget compatible with interactive
swarm control, and how is that budget distributed across stages?
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ3 (Acoustic robustness and control quality).} How do command recognition rate and word
error rate degrade as SNR falls from clean to 5~dB under propeller-type noise, does the system
degrade \emph{safely} rather than \emph{wrongly}, and does the controller hold formation to
specification?
\end{quote}

The second clause of RQ2 carries as much weight as the first. A pipeline that meets its budget
without an account of where the time went has demonstrated one configuration on one device and
established nothing about which stage would break first under a longer utterance, a slower model
or a warmer processor; per-stage attribution is what turns a passing measurement into a result
that transfers, and it is why NFR-3 instruments every stage rather than only the endpoints. RQ3 is
similarly three questions in one sentence, and the middle one is the one this document was built
around. Degradation under noise is expected and is measured as a curve; what
Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} committed the architecture to is the \emph{direction} that curve
takes as it falls, which is a separate measurement from the rate itself. The third clause is
deliberately answered by a separate experiment: formation quality is measured on its own rather
than inside the acoustic trials, because a single experiment varying both the signal-to-noise
ratio and the controller's workload would confound speech-recognition performance with control
performance and leave neither interpretable.

The remaining research question, on the accuracy--efficiency trade-off across small language
models quantised for CPU-only execution, is pursued by the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}. This
document consumes its answer --- the selected model and quantisation level --- as a fixed input to
the runtime described here.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
One of the project's four claimed contributions belongs to this document.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C4.} A dual-path runtime in which a two-class keyword spotter provides a bounded
    safety reflex that preempts language-model inference, with the membership rule for that path
    derived from a latency argument rather than chosen by preference.
\end{itemize}

The transferable part of C4 is the second clause. A fast path that bypasses validation is a
familiar pattern, and a system can readily be given one; what is easy to omit is a principled
answer to which utterances may travel on it, with the result that the path grows by convenience
until it carries the commands it was never safe to carry. The rule derived in
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} answers that question from the cost of a false accept rather than
from preference, it yields exactly two classes for this vocabulary, and it states the condition
under which a third could ever be admitted --- which is what makes it auditable by someone who did
not write it, and what makes it apply to systems other than this one.

The engineering realised in this document is considerably larger than the contribution it claims.
A complete offline voice pipeline, a validated command bus, a flight state machine, a swarm
controller with two interchangeable simulation backends and a live demonstration are the substance
of the chapters that follow, and none of them is claimed as novel: assembling known components
into a system that runs within a measured budget on a fixed device is engineering work, and
reporting it as a research contribution would inflate the claim. The three remaining contributions
--- the frozen command schema with its matched grammar, the label-first dataset pipeline, and the
measured accuracy--latency--memory comparison across models and quantisation levels --- are
claimed by the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}.

\section{Structure of this document}
\label{sec:structure}
Five chapters follow. Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art} reviews voice-controlled unmanned
aerial vehicle systems and positions this work against the natural-language drone-control agent of
Lim et al.~\cite{lim2025}, which is the project's named baseline.
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} presents the design: the dual-path decomposition, the Branch~A
membership rule derived rather than asserted, the three validation layers, the stage-by-stage
latency budget and the allocation of a four-core processor between paths that must not starve one
another. Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} describes what was built --- the audio chain, the
runtime and its command bus, the flight state machine, the swarm controller and both simulation
backends. Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} reports the three experiments that test the design against
the requirements of Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}: preemption and latency under load,
end-to-end accuracy as the signal-to-noise ratio falls, and formation quality, each with its
statistical analysis. Chapter~\ref{chap:demonstration} presents the live demonstration, states the
limitations the measurements expose, and closes with the work this system leaves to be done.

Stated as a single engineering problem: a spoken command must reach five aircraft quickly enough
to be useful and must be stopped by the same voice quickly enough to matter, with every route from
the microphone to the vehicle bounded by a check placed where the action occurs rather than where
it is requested. The chapters that follow report how far that was built, how much of it the
measurements support, and where they fall short of what was committed here.
