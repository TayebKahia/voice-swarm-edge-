\section{The edge-inference problem}
\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
A drone that answers to spoken commands is only as autonomous as its network connection allows it
to be, unless the language model that turns speech into a command lives on the aircraft itself.
This work imposes that constraint at the outset: every stage of command parsing, from the
transcript to the validated structured command, runs on a Raspberry Pi 5, with no network access
at any point in the pipeline. Removing the network removes cloud latency and removes a channel by
which data could leave the aircraft, but it also removes the option of falling back on a large
model when a small one struggles. The inference budget has to be closed entirely on the
\gls{cpu} of an edge board, against language models whose usual home is a data-centre
accelerator.

That difficulty is not unique to this hardware or this task. A recent benchmark of twenty-five
quantised language models on three \glspl{sbc} finds that such boards reliably support models
of up to about 1.5~billion parameters, with larger models dropping to a few tokens per
second~\cite{sbc2025}; Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art} takes up that literature in full. Here the
same constraint is stated for one target device and three deployment candidates,
Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, SmolLM2-360M-Instruct and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, with a fourth,
parameter-matched model fine-tuned as a control (Chapter~\ref{chap:method}). On this device the
constraint takes the form of a measurable throughput margin. A structured command in this system
is short: a 22-token decode is budgeted to complete within 1{,}100~ms, which sets a throughput
floor of at least 20~tokens per second.
Measured on that target, restricted to the three cores the deployed configuration reserves for
speech recognition and language-model inference, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct at Q4\_K\_M clears the
floor at 27.93~tok/s and Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct does not, at 14.53~tok/s
(Chapter~\ref{chap:results}). The margin is real, but it is not a property of the model alone.
The same Qwen configuration, on the same board and the same three cores, decodes at 18.13~tok/s
when the board is left to throttle -- below the floor it clears by 40\% once an active cooler
holds the clock. A throughput figure on an \gls{sbc} is a statement about an enclosure as much as
about a model, which is why every timing figure in this document is reported with the thermal
state it was measured under.

This is the edge-inference problem this thesis addresses. The question is not whether a
\gls{slm} can be made accurate enough for the task, but whether an accurate-enough one can
also be made to fit on hardware whose available compute depends on its thermal state.

\section{Why structured output matters for robot control}
Accuracy is not the only property this system needs from its language model, and on its own it is
not even the useful one. A flight controller downstream does not read prose; it reads a fixed
\gls{json} schema, and it cannot act on a partially well-formed command. A model that is right
about the intent and the slot values but wrong about the syntax has produced nothing a controller
can use, so structural correctness has to be treated as a precondition, not as a component folded
into an accuracy score. That an aggregate accuracy figure can conceal what a model actually does is
a point the compression literature makes independently: a model and its compressed counterpart can
score alike on a benchmark yet differ substantially in which individual answers they get
right~\cite{dutta2024}.

A preliminary zero-shot run on the target board illustrates why this precondition can be left
neither to the model alone nor to an under-specified grammar. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct at Q4\_K\_M,
without fine-tuning, was decoded greedily with a 48-token cap under an early draft of the grammar,
on five commands. It reproduced the emergency-abort command exactly. It addressed the collective
command ``swarm takeoff'' to a single drone: a well-formed output with the wrong meaning, since
which drones a command addresses is a question no grammar can settle. On two commands, including
``swarm land'', it looped on drone identifiers until the token cap cut the output off, because the
draft grammar placed no upper bound on the length of the identifier list. The truncated outputs
failed to parse and resolved to a hover, so the landing was not executed.

Neither failure is an occasional slip to be tolerated at some acceptable rate: a mis-addressed
collective command moves the wrong aircraft, and a lost landing command leaves the swarm hovering
when it was told to land. The two failures call for different remedies. The loop is a structural
defect, and it motivates a schema-as-contract design: a bounded grammar constrains what the model
can emit by construction, so that structural validity stops being a property measured after the
fact and becomes a property of the decoder itself. The grammar of Section~\ref{sec:schema} bounds
the identifier list at five entries, which removes the loop. The mis-addressed command lies beyond
the reach of any grammar; it is the task of fine-tuning, and it is what the accuracy evaluation
measures. The first remedy is the first contribution of this document, stated below.

\section{Objectives}
This work is part of a system that converts spoken English commands into validated structured
commands for a swarm of five drones, entirely on the Raspberry Pi 5. An operator says, for example,
``form a circle with radius five metres'': a \gls{stt} model transcribes the utterance, a fine-tuned
\gls{slm} parses the transcript into a \gls{json} command under the grammar, a validator checks it,
and the swarm controller, which runs with the simulation on a workstation linked to the board,
executes it. Ten intents cover the vocabulary, from take-off and landing
to formations and parameter changes. Two safety phrases, ``swarm hold'' and ``swarm abort'', are
also recognised by a keyword spotter that runs no language model at all. The speech is real,
recorded through the deployed microphone, but the aircraft are simulated: the controller drives a
software-in-the-loop simulation of five vehicles, and no physical flight is claimed.
Reinforcement learning, vision input and languages other than English are outside the scope.
The work is divided between two theses: this one is the research half, which selects and
evaluates the language model, and the companion \emph{M\'emoire d'Ing\'enieur} is the
engineering half, which builds the runtime around it.

This document pursues one research question, stated in full because everything in the chapters
that follow answers to it:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ1 (Accuracy--efficiency trade-off).} What is the trade-off across small language models
of 0.36--1.2~B parameters, fine-tuned for drone-swarm command parsing and quantised for
CPU-only execution on a Raspberry Pi 5?
\end{quote}

The two remaining research questions, on pipeline latency and on acoustic robustness and control
quality, are pursued by the \emph{M\'emoire d'Ing\'enieur}, together with the engineering
architecture, the runtime implementation, and the live demonstration built on the model this
document selects.

\section{Contributions}
Three contributions belong to this document.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{C1.} A frozen command schema paired with a matched, bounded \gls{gbnf}\glsadd{ggml}
    grammar~\cite{llamacpp}, built so that structural validity is a property of the decoder
    rather than a rate measured against a test set. The grammar is the first layer of a
    three-layer validation stack; the remaining two, a semantic validator and a state machine,
    are specified in the \emph{M\'emoire d'Ing\'enieur}.
    \item \textbf{C2.} A label-first dataset pipeline, built and run end to end from natural-language
    transcripts to swarm-control primitives, with splits enforced at the template-family level,
    round-trip audio augmentation, and a dataset card documenting the result. The dataset is
    frozen under a version tag, and the author's first annotation pass is sealed under a SHA-256
    checksum, so that a later edit to either is detected rather than trusted not to happen.
    \item \textbf{C3.} A quantised-inference harness that measures \gls{em}, intent and slot
    F1, schema validity, false-command rate and safe-failure rate for three of the four fine-tuned
    models at two
    quantisation levels, with accuracy scored under the deployed runtime and grammar, and
    throughput timed on the Raspberry Pi 5. It measures the accuracy cost of the deployed pipeline
    on this task at this scale, rather than inheriting it from results obtained on larger models
    and generic benchmarks. Because weight precision, runtime and grammar change together between
    the reference and the deployed configuration, that cost belongs to the pipeline as a whole, not
    to quantisation alone. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} presents what it measured.
\end{itemize}

The fourth contribution of the joint work, the dual-path runtime that lets a keyword spotter
preempt language-model inference for safety-critical commands, belongs to the \emph{M\'emoire
d'Ing\'enieur}.

\section{Structure of this document}
The rest of this document has six chapters in three parts. Part~I holds
Chapter~\ref{chap:background}, which sets out the foundations the document relies on: language
models and their adaptation, quantisation, inference on a single-board computer,
grammar-constrained decoding, the intent-and-slot formulation of the task, and the metrics and
tests used to score it. Part~II holds Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art}, which reviews the
studies that have measured language models under each of those conditions, compares them to one
another, and states the gap this work fills. Part~III is the contribution. Chapter~\ref{chap:method}
sets out the command schema and grammar, the dataset construction, the fine-tuning recipe, the
quantisation procedure, and the evaluation protocol. Chapter~\ref{chap:results} reports the
speaker-sensitivity experiment, the multi-model benchmark, a parameter-matched control that
measures a model-family difference at fixed size, the quantisation delta, and the grammar ablation, with
the statistical analysis and the selection rule applied to them. Chapter~\ref{chap:discussion}
discusses the accuracy--efficiency trade-off this comparison exposes, the failure modes observed
along the way, and the threats to the validity of the comparison itself.
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} closes with a direct answer to RQ1 and the work this comparison
leaves for later.

The chapters that follow measure, for each deployment candidate, the throughput it sustains
against the 20~tok/s floor under the fixed schema, and the accuracy it retains after quantisation.
