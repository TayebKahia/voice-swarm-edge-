\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
This chapter sets out the operational context the system is built for, the requirements it is held
to and the safety problem that shapes its architecture. It then states the two research questions
and the contribution this document pursues, and the structure of the chapters that follow.

\section{Operational context}
\label{sec:operational-context}
The system described in this document places one operator in front of five aircraft and gives
that operator a single control surface: speech. The formation is addressed as a unit --- an
utterance such as ``form a circle with radius five metres'' commands five vehicles at once, where
a manual interface would require either five sets of stick inputs or a ground-control station the
operator has to look down at. The unit of command is therefore the formation rather than the
vehicle, and the operator's attention stays on the airspace instead of on a screen. That is the
first constraint the system inherits, and it is an interface constraint before it is a modelling
one.

The second constraint comes from where the operator stands. The deployment setting is an outdoor
site chosen for the flight rather than for its connectivity: no operator-controlled access point,
no assurance of cellular coverage, and no prepared infrastructure of any kind. A pipeline stage
that reaches the network is consequently a stage whose availability is a property of the site and
not of the system, and no requirement can be placed on it that the site is able to violate.

Offline operation is therefore a requirement and not a preference, for three reasons, each of
which independently favours it. Availability is the first, as above. Latency is the second: a
remote call inserts a tail the system does not control into a loop that must hold a
95th-percentile (p95) budget, and a percentile budget is defensible only when the tail of every
stage in it is under local control (Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}). The safety path
is the third and the most binding. The two spoken phrases that stop the swarm have to remain
available precisely when other parts of the deployment have failed, and a stopping command that
depends on infrastructure the site may not provide is not a dependable stop.
Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements} states offline operation as a \gls{nfr}, verified by an
end-to-end run with networking disabled. Offline here means independent of any infrastructure the
site does not provide. The wireless link from the device to the vehicles, which in this work joins
the device to the workstation running the simulation, belongs to the system: its availability is
the system's responsibility rather than the site's, and it lies outside this requirement.

What that decision puts on the device is the entire chain. The keyword spotter, the \gls{vad}
model, the \gls{stt} model, the language model, the validator and the swarm controller all execute
on one Raspberry Pi~5 with 8~GB of memory, sharing four Cortex-A76 cores with each other and with
the operating system. The consequence for model selection is the subject of the \emph{M\'emoire de
Master}, which owns that comparison: which \glspl{slm} can meet a throughput floor on this device
at all, and at what accuracy. The present document takes the configuration selected there as given
and is concerned with the runtime built around it.

That runtime divides the chain into two paths that share the microphone. On the \emph{reflex
path}, the keyword spotter maps two stopping phrases directly to a hold or an abort, without
transcription or parsing. On the \emph{parse path}, the voice-activity detector, the
speech-recognition model and the language model turn a complete utterance into a structured
command. Commands from both paths are checked by the validator and the flight state machine before
they reach the swarm controller. A \emph{hold} is the \texttt{hover} command: lateral motion
stopped, altitude held, rotors running, and the mission recoverable.

One boundary of the operational context must be stated at the outset, because every claim in this
document is scoped by it: the aircraft are simulated. The controller drives a networked
software-in-the-loop simulation of $N = 5$ vehicles at 50~Hz, through a single environment
interface with two interchangeable backends, the PyFlyt simulator~\cite{pyflyt} and a kinematic
simulator. The audio path is not simulated --- the evaluation corpus, referred to below as the
golden set, is recorded by one speaker through the deployed microphone in a real room, and the
demonstration runs from a live microphone --- but the flight is. Results reported here therefore
characterise the command pipeline and the controller, the latter on the kinematic simulator
without rotor dynamics (Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}), not airframe behaviour in wind, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:demonstration} returns to this as a limitation rather than leaving it implicit.

\section{Engineering requirements}
\label{sec:engineering-requirements}
Every requirement in this work is stated with its verification method, so that each can be checked
against a measurement. Twelve \glspl{fr} and twenty-one \glspl{nfr} govern the system as a
whole, and they are shared between this document and the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}. The subset
stated here is not a matter of editorial judgement: this document answers the second and third
research questions of Section~\ref{sec:objectives}, on pipeline latency (RQ2) and on acoustic
robustness and control quality (RQ3), so it carries the requirements those two questions resolve
to. The remainder are stated, verified and discussed by the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}, which
answers the first question (RQ1), on the accuracy--efficiency trade-off across \glspl{slm} on the
target device. Table~\ref{tab:functional-requirements} lists the \glspl{fr} this document carries
and Table~\ref{tab:nonfunctional-requirements} the non-functional ones, each under a short name
that the later chapters use to refer to it; a criterion shared with the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}
carries the same name there. Three experiments, reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}, produce
the measurements. The \emph{latency experiment} times every stage of both paths on the Raspberry
Pi~5, measures the reflex path both idle and while the parse path is decoding, and trials the

\emph{acoustic-robustness experiment} runs the full pipeline on the golden-set recordings with
propeller noise mixed in at a falling \gls{snr}. The \emph{formation-control experiment}

accuracy, collisions and every intervention of the separation clamp. Every target below is a

architecture before any component is chosen. The reflex budget and the parse budget are not one
budget with two tolerances, and they are not anchored at the same instant: reflex latency is
measured from the keyword offset, end-to-end latency from end-of-speech. A path that transcribes and parses a complete
utterance cannot begin until the endpointer has confirmed that the operator has stopped speaking,
a wait that alone exceeds 150~ms, and transcription and decoding then each take of the order of a
second on this device (Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture}). A command that must act within 150~ms of
its keyword offset therefore cannot travel on the parse path, however that path is tuned; it needs
a path that neither transcribes nor parses. The reflex-latency criterion also carries a condition
that makes it an architectural requirement rather than a benchmark: it must hold \emph{while} the parse path is
decoding, which is a statement about scheduling and core allocation on a four-core device, not
about the spotter in isolation. Preemption recovery closes the same loop from the other end: it
bounds the time after a reflex before the parse path can accept a new utterance.

\section{The safety problem}
\label{sec:safety-problem}
In a system that turns speech into vehicle motion, the two ways of being wrong are not equally
expensive. A command that fails to be acted on costs the operator a repetition. A command that is
acted on wrongly moves five aircraft, and it does so with the same authority as a correct one,
because nothing downstream of the parser can tell a well-formed, in-range and state-legal wrong
command from a correct one. The failure surface is wide enough to make this more than a remote
concern. Four errors are possible: the \gls{stt} stage can mishear a word, the parser can assign
the wrong intent to a correctly transcribed sentence, a slot value can be recovered with the wrong
magnitude or sign, and a command correct in isolation can arrive while the swarm is in a state
that cannot accept it. The design objective that follows is not the minimisation of the error
rate, which is the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}'s concern, but control over the \emph{direction} of
the errors that remain. A misheard command must not become motion. Treating failure modes as
unequal in consequence is built into the dependability taxonomy, which grades failures by severity
from minor to catastrophic~\cite{avizienis2004}, and systems-theoretic hazard analysis designs
against the worst-case loss rather than its estimated likelihood~\cite{stpa}. What this section
derives is not that principle but its consequence for a speech-driven flight path, where the stage
that produces the error is a language model and the stage that acts on it cannot detect one that
passes its checks.

An accuracy target on its own does not express that objective, because an accuracy figure is
silent about what the failures do. Two systems scoring identically on \gls{em} can differ
completely in consequence, one resolving an ambiguous utterance to a hold and the other to a
heading change, and the score that ranks them equal is measuring the wrong property for a
flight-control path. The architecture therefore places three layers between a spoken utterance and
the vehicle: a grammar that constrains the decoder so that every completed decode is structurally
valid, a semantic validator that enforces the meaning constraints a context-free grammar cannot
express, and a flight state machine that admits a command only if the swarm's current state can
accept it. The first layer is specified in the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}, which owns the schema
and grammar; the second and third belong to this document and are designed in
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture}. What makes them a safety mechanism rather than a filter is the
exit they share: a rejection never becomes a substituted or best-guess movement. Output the
validator cannot accept, including output the parser fails to complete, resolves to a hold with a
log entry, as does a command the state machine rejects while the swarm is taking off or in flight;
where the flight state does not admit a hold --- on the ground, during a descent and after an
abort --- the rejection is a logged no-op instead. The dependability taxonomy calls a system that
fails only in such specified modes fail-controlled, and one whose failures are, to an acceptable
extent, all minor, fail-safe~\cite{avizienis2004}; the hold and the no-op are how this
architecture seeks the second property through the first, and what is specific to it is that no
rejection path anywhere in the stack can produce motion.

Those three layers protect against commands that are wrong. They do nothing about commands that
are late, and the distinction matters most for the one command that exists to prevent an accident
already in progress. An operator who sees the formation begin to do something unintended and says
\emph{swarm hold} is making a request whose value decays with every millisecond it spends in a
queue. Routed through the parse path, that request inherits the parse path's cost. It waits for
the utterance to end, then for transcription, then for a language model to decode a structured
command, and then for the validation layers to admit it. That budget is 2{,}500~ms at p95
(Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}), and its clock does not start until the operator has
stopped speaking. A stopping command on the parse path therefore has the latency of a movement
command, which is the wrong design for the only utterance in the vocabulary whose purpose is to
arrest motion.

The response is to give that utterance its own path. A two-class keyword spotter listens
continuously and reacts to two phrases, \texttt{swarm hold} and \texttt{swarm abort}, without
transcription and without invoking the language model at all, preempting an in-flight decode if
one is running. The reflex is budgeted at 150~ms at p95 from the keyword offset
(Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}), and the
budget must hold \emph{while} the language model is decoding, which is what makes the dual path an
argument about processor allocation rather than a second copy of the same pipeline. The design is
the contribution this document claims (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}), and
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} derives it in full.

The reflex path introduces a hazard of its own, and the membership rule that governs it is the
part of this design that most repays explanation. A phrase admitted to the reflex path reaches the
swarm without transcription or parsing, the two stages that establish what the operator said. The
validator and the state machine still check the command it publishes, but a spurious trigger
publishes a command that is well-formed, within the physical envelope and, while the swarm is in
flight, legal, so that no check in the architecture can distinguish it from one the operator
spoke; speed is bought by removing the only stages that could have noticed the error. A path whose
false accepts no check can detect may therefore carry only commands whose incorrect execution moves
the swarm towards a safer state, at a cost to the mission rather than to safety, and in a flight
vocabulary the commands meeting that condition are precisely those that remove energy from the
system. Of those, only the commands whose effect depends on arriving within the time the reflex
saves need the reflex at all; a landing, whose descent is itself slow, does not. A spurious
\texttt{swarm hold} costs an unnecessary hold, recoverable by speaking again. A movement verb
admitted to the same path --- a phrase as ordinary as \emph{swarm forward} --- would on a false
accept produce uncommanded motion that every check in the architecture would pass, which is the
failure the validation layers exist to prevent, reached through the path added to stop the swarm
faster. The membership rule is consequently derived from the latency argument rather than chosen
by preference, it admits exactly two classes, and it is closed to any command that adds motion: no
movement verb may be added later without invalidating the reason the reflex path is permitted to
skip interpretation.

Command-level correctness is still not the whole of the safety problem, because a stream of
correct commands can produce an unsafe trajectory. Five aircraft converging on assigned formation
slots have crossing paths by construction. Separation between them is maintained by an artificial
potential field, a soft mechanism that shapes the velocity field so that vehicles repel one
another. With discrete timesteps and bounded actuation, a vehicle can still cross a separation
boundary within a single integration step. Potential fields also have failure modes identified as
inherent to the method~\cite{koren1991}: trap situations at local minima where attractive and
repulsive terms cancel, oscillation near obstacles and in narrow passages, and --- the mode that
bears most directly on a formation whose slots are close together --- no passage between closely
spaced obstacles, where the combined repulsion exceeds the attraction even though the gap admits
the vehicle. The architecture therefore adds a hard geometric separation clamp at the integrator
--- at the point where position is actually updated, rather than where motion is requested. The
placement mirrors the flight state machine one level up: at the command level the last word
belongs to a check immediately before the command takes effect, and at the trajectory level to a
check immediately before the position does. In both cases the final check is independent of the
stage that produced its input.

That placement determines what this document may claim, and the claim is stated once here in the
form it keeps throughout. The commitment is zero inter-drone collisions \emph{observed} across all
trials, backed by a hard geometric separation clamp at the integrator. It is not a claim that no
collision could occur, and it is not a claim that the potential field alone maintains separation.
The difference between those sentences is the difference between a statement about the trials
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} reports and a statement about every state the controller could ever
reach; the first is what an experiment can establish, and the second would require a proof over
the reachable set that this work does not attempt. The weaker sentence is also the more
informative one, because it names the mechanism the result rests on instead of attributing the
result to a method that cannot supply it. Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} accordingly reports,
beside the collision count, the number of clamp interventions: the vehicle pairs, summed over
timesteps, that the potential field alone had brought inside the clamp distance. That count is
what makes the observed figure evidence of a working mechanism rather than evidence that the
mechanism was never needed.

The safety problem, stated once and carried through the rest of this document, is therefore
threefold: a wrong command must be hard to act on, a stopping command must be fast to act on, and
the execution of any command must be bounded at the point where it takes effect.
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} designs the first two mechanisms and
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} realises all three, the third as part of the swarm controller;
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} measures whether each holds.

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}
The work reported in the two documents poses three research questions, of which this document
pursues two. Both concern the system as an operating whole rather than any component within it,
and both are stated here in full because the chapters that follow are organised to answer them.
The pipeline they refer to is the chain named in Section~\ref{sec:operational-context}: a keyword
spotter, a \gls{vad} stage, an \gls{stt} stage, a grammar-constrained \gls{slm}, the validator,
and the swarm controller.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ2 (Pipeline latency).} Can a fully offline pipeline (keyword spotter + \gls{vad} +
\gls{stt} + grammar-constrained \gls{slm} + validator + controller) meet a p95 budget compatible
with interactive swarm control, and how is that budget distributed across stages?
\end{quote}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{RQ3 (Acoustic robustness and control quality).} How do \acrlong{crr} and \acrlong{wer}
degrade as \gls{snr} falls from clean to 5~dB under propeller-type noise, does the system degrade
\emph{safely} rather than \emph{wrongly}, and does the controller hold formation to specification?
\end{quote}

The second clause of RQ2 carries as much weight as the first. A pipeline that meets its budget
without an account of where the time went has demonstrated one configuration on one device. It
establishes nothing about which stage would fail first under a longer utterance, a slower model or
a warmer processor. Per-stage attribution is what makes a passing measurement transferable, and it
is why the per-stage attribution criterion instruments every stage rather than only the
endpoints. RQ3 is similarly three
questions in one sentence, and the middle one is the one this document was built around.
Degradation under noise is expected and is measured as a curve; what
Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem} commits the architecture to is the \emph{direction} that curve
takes as it falls, measured as the safe-failure rate named in
Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}, which is a separate measurement from the rate itself.
The third clause is deliberately answered by a separate experiment: formation quality is measured
on its own rather than inside the acoustic trials, because a single experiment varying both the
\gls{snr} and the controller's workload would confound speech-recognition performance with control
performance and leave neither interpretable.

The remaining research question (RQ1), on the accuracy--efficiency trade-off across \glspl{slm}
quantised for execution on the \gls{cpu} alone, is pursued by the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}. This
document consumes its answer --- the selected model and quantisation level --- as a fixed input to
the runtime described here.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}
Of the four contributions claimed across the two documents, one belongs to this document; the
other three, named at the end of this section, belong to the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Contribution.} A dual-path runtime in which a two-class keyword spotter provides a bounded
safety reflex that preempts language-model inference, with the membership rule for that path
derived from a latency argument rather than chosen by preference.
\end{quote}

% NOTE: the dual-attention wake-word transducer (arXiv:2304.01905) was checked and
%       deliberately NOT cited here: it switches compute paths inside a single model
%       rather than running two stages in parallel, so it does not support this claim.
The transferable part of this contribution is its second clause. Running a fast path beside a slower one is a
familiar pattern: arrangements that pair a fast responsive path with a slower, higher-quality one,
with a gate or a cancellable commitment between them, recur in real-time spoken
dialogue~\cite{relays2s,mira}. What is easy to omit is a principled answer to which utterances may
travel on the fast path; without one, commands can be added to it for convenience, including
commands whose false accept would produce motion. The rule derived in
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} answers that question from the value of the time the path saves
and the cost of a false accept rather than from preference, and it yields exactly two classes for
this vocabulary. It also states the condition under which a third class could be admitted, which
is what makes it auditable by someone who did not write it, and what makes it apply to systems
other than this one.

The engineering realised in this document is larger than the contribution it claims. A complete
offline voice pipeline, a validated command bus, a flight state machine, a swarm controller with
two interchangeable simulation backends and a live demonstration are the substance of the chapters
that follow. None of them is claimed as novel: assembling known components into a system that runs
within a measured budget on a fixed device is engineering work, and reporting it as a research
contribution would overstate the claim. The three remaining contributions --- the frozen command
schema with its matched grammar, the label-first dataset pipeline, and the quantised-inference
harness that measures the accuracy cost of the deployed pipeline --- are claimed by the
\emph{M\'emoire de Master}.

\section{Structure of this document}
\label{sec:structure}
Five chapters follow. Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art} reviews voice-controlled \gls{uav}
systems and positions this work against the natural-language drone-control agent of Lim
et al.~\cite{lim2025}, which is the baseline against which this work is compared.
Chapter~\ref{chap:architecture} presents the design: the dual-path decomposition, the membership
rule of the reflex path derived rather than asserted, the three validation layers, the
stage-by-stage latency budget and the allocation of a four-core processor between paths that must
not starve one another. Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} describes what was built --- the audio
chain, the runtime and its command bus, the flight state machine, the swarm controller and both
simulation backends. Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} reports the three experiments that test the
design against the requirements of Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}: the latency
experiment, on preemption and latency under load; the acoustic-robustness experiment, on
end-to-end accuracy as the \gls{snr} falls; and the formation-control experiment, on formation
quality, each with its statistical analysis. Chapter~\ref{chap:demonstration} presents the live
demonstration, states the limitations the measurements expose, and closes with the work this
system leaves to be done.
