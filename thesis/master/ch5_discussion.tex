\chapter{Discussion and limitations}
\label{chap:discussion}

This chapter interprets what Chapter~\ref{chap:results} measured. Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff}
reads the accuracy--efficiency trade-off of RQ1 from the Pareto frontier of
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, and asks how much of that trade-off reaches the deployment decision.
Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes} classifies the parser's errors by what each would have dispatched,
and proposes one hypothesis linking the grammar ablation to the absence of in-domain abstention.
Section~\ref{sec:threats} examines whether the differences the comparison reports have the causes
they are attributed to, and how far they carry beyond the conditions under which they were
measured. The chapter's readings are held to the strength the data support, and several are stated as
hypotheses that the present data cannot test. RQ1 itself is decided in
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}, not here.

\section{The accuracy--efficiency trade-off}
\label{sec:tradeoff}

RQ1 was posed as a trade-off: across the 0.36--1.2~B range, how much task accuracy does a smaller
model give up in exchange for latency and memory? Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} places the measured
configurations for this task and this board, and this section examines how much of the trade-off
on the figure reaches the deployment decision.

\paragraph{Composition of the frontier.} Only three configurations have both an accuracy and a
latency coordinate, and one of them, Llama-3.2-1B at Q4\_K\_M, is dominated. The frontier is
therefore the pair \{SmolLM2-360M, Qwen2.5-0.5B\}, and a pair defines a direction, not a curve.
Moving along it from Qwen2.5-0.5B to SmolLM2-360M saves 178~ms of combined \gls{slm} p95
(1{,}072~ms to 894~ms) and 0.13~GiB of peak resident memory, and costs 17.5~pp of \gls{em} on the
golden split. That accuracy cost is one the paired tests establish as significant
(Section~\ref{sec:statistics}), unlike the difference in the other direction. Moving the other way, from Qwen2.5-0.5B to
Llama-3.2-1B, adds 1{,}210~ms and 0.95~GiB, and the accuracy difference it buys is not
distinguishable from zero on this evidence. Read together, the two directions suggest that,
among the models measured, accuracy stops rising at Qwen2.5-0.5B: the one smaller model loses
17.5~pp, and the one larger costs latency and memory for no difference these tests can detect.
With three points on the latency axis this is a reading of the figure's shape, not a measured
property of the size range, and it should be held at that strength.

\paragraph{Model-specific position of the knee.} It would be tempting to restate the
previous paragraph as ``0.5~B parameters suffice''. The iso-parameter control of
Section~\ref{sec:iso-parameter} argues against that restatement. H2O-Danube3-500M, within 4.0\% of
Qwen2.5-0.5B's parameter count, scores 6.5~pp lower on the golden split at FP16. It has no latency
coordinate, because it has no quantised artefact, so it cannot be placed on
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}, and the comparison has to be made between the unquantised models. At FP16
the two frontier models are 16.0~pp apart (0.935 against 0.775), so at a fixed size the vertical
position of a point moved by more than a third of the distance separating them. Part of both gaps
may reflect convergence rather than family: no model is shown to have converged within the
three-epoch budget, and the lower-scoring model of each pair improved most in the final epoch
(Section~\ref{sec:iso-parameter}). The frontier's knee
therefore sits at Qwen2.5-0.5B specifically, and whether another family at the same size would sit
on it, above it or below it is not something these data decide. A practitioner reusing this result
should read it as a statement about a model that was measured, not about a parameter class.

\paragraph{Frontier and selection rule.} The rule of Section~\ref{sec:selection} deploys the
configuration with the highest \gls{em} among those satisfying the end-to-end latency budget and the
memory ceiling. Latency and memory enter it as constraints and accuracy as the only ranking
criterion, so a Pareto frontier offers the rule no operating point to trade along: every
configuration satisfying the constraints is ranked on accuracy alone, and latency returns only to
break a tie. That is what happened here. Under the reading most favourable to the faster model,
which admits SmolLM2-360M although it was not measured end to end, the rule still selects
Qwen2.5-0.5B, since 0.935 against 0.760 is a difference the paired tests establish as significant.
That SmolLM2-360M also falls 9~pp short of the 0.85 exact-match threshold of
Table~\ref{tab:requirements} is a second fact about it, not the reason it is not selected. Of the
three measured configurations, then, one is dominated, one loses the ranking, and one remains, and
it remains only against the re-baselined constraints: against the 2{,}500~ms budget as specified,
Qwen2.5-0.5B misses by 622~ms (25\%). RQ1's trade-off exists on the figure, but under a rule of this
form it does not reach the deployment decision. This is a consequence of the rule's form as much as
of which three models were measured. A faster model would change the selection only by matching or
exceeding Qwen2.5-0.5B's accuracy, and a rule that priced latency against accuracy could select a
different point from the same figure; nothing measured here rules out either possibility.

\paragraph{Thermal dependence of the latency axis.} The latency coordinates in
Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} are measured on a cooled board, and Table~\ref{tab:thermal-headroom} reports
how far they move without the cooler. Uncooled, the selected configuration's combined \gls{slm} p95
is 1{,}771.5~ms. That is over the 1{,}350~ms stage allowance of Table~\ref{tab:latency-budget}, and
so is SmolLM2-360M's 1{,}610.2~ms, so on the uncooled run no configuration fits the language-model
stages inside their allowance. Cooled, the selected configuration fits the combined and decode
allowances, with modest margins, but not the 250~ms prefill allowance (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).
The combined stage is 278~ms inside its allowance, a margin that holds under cached prefill only,
since the deployed parser's uncached prefill p95 is 751~ms; decode alone is 1{,}033~ms against
1{,}100~ms, a margin of 67~ms or 6\%. The stage allowance is a line of the latency budget and not a
constraint of the selection rule, so none of this decides feasibility. Against the rule's own
end-to-end budget the selected configuration misses as specified, and that figure was measured on
the cooled board. What the comparison does suggest is that on this class of hardware the enclosure
behaves as a term in the latency budget, alongside the model. The figure is best read as the
frontier of a cooled Raspberry~Pi~5, and a latency or throughput figure quoted without its thermal
state may not be reproducible.

\paragraph{Unmeasured points and axes.} Two gaps in the figure are known, and each limits what
the frontier can be taken to mean. The first is quantisation. The Q8\_0 artefacts have no
latency coordinate, and the paired tests found no significant accuracy difference between
quantisations within any model. Where a Q8\_0 point would sit depends on its latency,
which was not measured, so the frontier is strictly a Q4\_K\_M frontier.
The second is the horizontal axis itself, which covers the \gls{slm} stages only. For
the selected configuration the full parse path was measured, and it misses the end-to-end
budget (Section~\ref{sec:selection}); for the other two it was not. The remaining stages run the
same software whichever model is loaded, which might suggest adding a common offset to all three
points and keeping the ordering along the frontier. That inference is not safe. The stages share
one board's processor, and a larger model could slow them through contention, so the end-to-end
figures of SmolLM2-360M and Llama-3.2-1B cannot be read off their stage times beyond the lower
bound Section~\ref{sec:selection} derives for Llama-3.2-1B. Whether the ordering survives end to
end is plausible but unmeasured.

\section{Failure-mode analysis}
\label{sec:failure-modes}

\Gls{em} counts an error once, whatever that error would have done to the swarm. For a
controller the more informative question is what each wrong output would have dispatched, and this
section reads the errors of the quantised, grammar-constrained configurations in those terms. The
analysis takes reference text as input, so it describes the parser and not the speech stage, and it
pools the six quantised artefacts, so it characterises the approach rather than any one
configuration.

\paragraph{Composition of the safe failures.} The grammar bounds what an output can look like; it
does not bound whether an output is given. The safe-failure rate of 0.0530 reported in
Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} is 28 errors out of 528, and the 28 are three different events. Twenty-four
are the model emitting \texttt{hover}, a command to hold position that is harmless here but is
indistinguishable from the model having heard a request to hold. Three are the semantic validator
replacing a \texttt{formation} command that lacks a slot its shape requires with a hold. One is the
model emitting \texttt{unknown} on an in-domain command, the only error in 3{,}540 decodes that took
the form of declining to answer. A non-zero safe-failure rate could be read as evidence that the system recognises when to
stop. This breakdown supports a weaker reading: the system can be stopped by a later layer, and some
of its errors happen to be harmless. The metric cannot make the distinction itself, because it is
defined over the dispatched action and not over where that action came from.

\paragraph{In-domain and out-of-domain abstention.} The parser does abstain, but apparently on a criterion other
than uncertainty. On the out-of-domain set, where every reference is \texttt{unknown}, the quantised
configurations abstain on 60.7\% to 81.3\% of items, the complement of the false-command rates
of Table~\ref{tab:false-command}. On the two in-domain splits the same
configurations make 282 errors, and \texttt{unknown} appears on an item whose reference is a real
command once in 2{,}640 decodes. That one case was itself an error: SmolLM2-360M declined a valid
hold command whose transcript carried a disfluency (``freeze, units fo--- four through five'').
The pattern suggests that what the models learned is closer to a classifier of request types than
to a confidence estimate. They separate off-topic
requests from supported ones more reliably than they separate commands they parsed correctly from
commands they did not. The distinction matters
in operation, because a misrecognised command still reads as a supported request, and it falls on the side of that boundary where, on the in-domain splits,
abstention is almost absent. Nor does the pattern appear to
belong to one model. At FP16 the three models of the main comparison abstain on 62.0\% to 73.3\% of
the out-of-domain set, and H2O-Danube3-500M, a fourth family trained under the same recipe, falls
inside that spread at 72.0\% (Table~\ref{tab:iso-parameter}).

\paragraph{Taxonomy of in-domain errors.} Nearly every in-domain error would have moved the swarm.
Of the 282, 240 (85.1\%) carry the right intent with a wrong parameter, 39 (13.8\%) carry a wrong
intent that is still actionable, and 3 (1.1\%) resolve to a safe action, so 98.9\% dispatch an
executable command (\texttt{results/limitation\_abstention.md}). The largest single family is a sign error on the yaw angle: 114 of the 282
errors (40.4\%) turn the swarm through the requested angle in the opposite direction. One instance
on the recorded golden set is the transcript ``heading 163.0 clockwise, number 1'', whose
reference yaw is $-163.0$ and for which Qwen2.5-0.5B at Q4\_K\_M emits $+163.0$. An error of this
kind is well-formed, inside the physical envelope and consistent with itself, so none of the three
validation layers of Section~\ref{sec:schema} has grounds to reject it: both signs are legal
values, and nothing within a single command distinguishes the direction the operator meant from its
opposite. Why this one parameter accounts for the largest share of errors is not established here.
The example suggests a candidate. The schema encodes direction as the sign of the angle, so a spoken
``clockwise'' has to become a minus sign that the transcript never contains, whereas a magnitude can
be copied from the transcript as spoken. That explanation is consistent with the quoted item and
has not been tested.

\paragraph{A common-cause hypothesis.} The grammar ablation of Section~\ref{sec:grammar-ablation}
and the abstention results admit a single reading, offered here as a hypothesis rather than a
finding. With the grammar removed, the fine-tuned models left the format once in 3{,}540 decodes and
\gls{em} moved by 0.17~pp, so the fine-tuned models already produced structurally valid output almost
without exception when no constraint was applied. The same training teaches \texttt{unknown} through 81 pairs, every one a request the
schema does not support, and through none in which a supported command was made unreadable
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset}): through what an utterance asks for, not through whether it can be read.
If the models learned both lessons with the
same thoroughness, the structural reliability and the abstention by request type are one effect of the
training data rather than two separate properties. The single malformed output is compatible with
this reading. It came from Llama-3.2-1B at Q4\_K\_M on the out-of-domain query ``what does lidar
stand for'', which it answered with a sentence of prose instead of a command, so the grammar's only
measurable contribution to validity fell where the training distribution ends. The hypothesis cannot be tested
on the present data. Every constrained and unconstrained decode uses the same fine-tuned weights, so
the contribution of the fine-tune cannot be separated from that of the grammar, and the untuned
baseline supports no causal reading (Section~\ref{sec:threats-internal}). Two interventions would
test it and would also address the failure: a confidence gate that routes decodes of low
log-probability to \texttt{unknown}, supplying the uncertainty signal the request-type boundary lacks, and
training pairs that map degraded or truncated commands to \texttt{unknown}. Neither was implemented
or measured in this work.

\section{Threats to validity}
\label{sec:threats}

The comparison of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} is open to two separate questions.
Section~\ref{sec:threats-internal} asks whether its differences have the causes they are
attributed to, and Section~\ref{sec:threats-external} asks how far they carry beyond the
conditions under which they were measured.

\subsection{Internal validity}
\label{sec:threats-internal}

Internal validity asks whether each difference reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} is caused
by the factor it is attributed to, a model, a quantisation level or the grammar, rather than by
something else in how the configurations were produced or measured. Some threats of this kind are
controlled by construction. Decoding is greedy, so a configuration returns the same output for the
same input and no difference between configurations is decoding noise. The chat-template parity
check of Section~\ref{sec:quantisation-procedure} passes the three deployed models on both the formatted text and the token identifiers, so a
difference between a reference model and its quantised artefact is not explained by a training and
serving tokenisation that disagree, on the ten audit prompts the check covers; that the same check fails H2O-Danube3-500M demonstrates that it can fail.
Checkpoints are selected on \texttt{val\_synth}, never on the golden set, so no figure of record was
tuned on the items that score it. Six threats remain.

\paragraph{The parameter-matched control.} Three declared differences separate H2O-Danube3-500M
from the models it is compared with (Section~\ref{sec:iso-parameter}). Its system prompt is folded
into the user turn, because its chat template has no system role. It was trained in a separate
session, and \gls{gpu} training is not guaranteed to be bit-reproducible across
sessions~\cite{pytorchrepro}. It exists
on the reference surface only. The first two could contribute to the 6.5~pp family difference, and
neither is known to act in a particular direction. The fold's effect may shrink over three epochs of
training, since the folded prompt becomes a fixed prefix the model learns, but it changes the input
the model was trained and evaluated on, and its effect was not measured. The third limits the
comparison rather than confounding it: the family difference is measured on the reference surface
for both models, not on the quantised artefacts that ship. The family difference is therefore a difference between two trained artefacts that
also differ in the first two respects, and it should be quoted with them. It was detected by an
exploratory exact McNemar test (15 against 2 discordant items, $p = 0.0023$).

\paragraph{Fixed training budget.} Every model was trained for three epochs, no model is shown to
have converged, and the lower-scoring model of each contrast improved most in the final epoch
(Section~\ref{sec:iso-parameter}). Part of both the SmolLM2-360M gap and the family gap may
therefore be convergence rather than capability, and a longer budget could narrow either. The
comparison is valid for the condition it measures, an identical three-epoch allowance, which is the
condition a practitioner with a fixed fine-tuning budget faces. It does not order the models by the
accuracy each could reach. Each model was also trained once, from one seed (Table~\ref{tab:lora}).
The paired tests account for the sampling of items, not for variation between training runs, which
was not measured, so a model or family difference is a difference between single trained adapters. A related qualification concerns precision: all four were trained in
fp16 although their checkpoints are released in bf16, and whether that substitution affected them
unequally is not ruled out (Section~\ref{sec:lora}).

\paragraph{The untuned baseline.} The four models score an \gls{em} of 0.000 on every split
when prompted zero-shot, and the predictions behind those rows were not retained. Two limits
follow. The misses cannot be split into malformed output and well-formed but wrong commands, so the
baseline does not show what the untuned models actually produce. And the zero-shot condition
differs from the deployed one in two respects at once, since it has neither fine-tuning nor the
grammar. It establishes that the accuracy of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} is not available from the
pretrained models as prompted; it does not attribute that accuracy to either intervention, which is
why the hypothesis of Section~\ref{sec:failure-modes} remains untested.

\paragraph{Attribution of the thermal effect.} The difference between the two benchmark runs is
attributed to the cooler, but the clock the uncooled run actually held was not logged, only its
1.5~GHz cap (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). The clock ratio between the runs is therefore at least
1.6$\times$, and SmolLM2-360M's 1.69$\times$ throughput gain is explained by clock frequency alone
only if the throttled clock fell below 1.42~GHz. Whether it did is unknown, and a remaining term,
if there is one, is unidentified. The comparison supports attributing the change to thermal state
as a whole, not to clock frequency specifically. It does not affect the selection, which uses the
cooled run only.

\paragraph{Sample size and dependence.} Accuracy of record rests on 200 golden items that are
paraphrases of 12 command patterns, and the paired tests treat them as independent. Items within a
pattern share whatever makes it easy or hard, so the effective sample is smaller than 200 and every
$p$-value is optimistic to an unestimated extent (Section~\ref{sec:definitions-of-record}). The
effect on the two model comparisons that matter differs. Qwen2.5-0.5B's advantage over
SmolLM2-360M, at $p = 1.1 \times 10^{-7}$ on 41 discordant pairs, sits more than five orders of
magnitude below the corrected threshold, so only a very large design effect would remove it. The
comparison with Llama-3.2-1B is the opposite case: five discordant items cannot reach the corrected
threshold under any split, so the absence of a significant difference there is a statement about
the test's power, not evidence that the two models are equally accurate. Latency figures carry a
parallel limit. Each p95 is taken over 60 timed repetitions, reduced from the 200 planned, so it is
set by the few slowest trials, and the 67~ms decode margin of Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff} could
be moved by a small number of additional slow trials.

\paragraph{Labels and evaluation splits.} The targets of record are generated, not annotated:
each label is written before any sentence exists, so annotator noise does not enter them. The
annotation check, which found no disagreement between two labelling passes, therefore bounds the
ambiguity of the schema rather than the correctness of the targets, and at the interval used its
null result is uninformative; it is not counted as evidence of reliability. The two in-domain
splits are also less independent than their two columns suggest. The golden transcripts were drawn
from the same 12 held-out template families as \texttt{test\_synth} (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}),
so the two splits measure the same command patterns in different phrasings, one as clean generated
text and one as the text the author read aloud. Agreement between them is consistency on those patterns, not two
independent confirmations, and neither split measures phrasings outside them; only the
out-of-domain set does.

\subsection{External validity}
\label{sec:threats-external}
External validity asks how far the results of Chapter~\ref{chap:results} carry beyond the
conditions they were measured under: reference text rather than speech, one board and one runtime
build, one speaker, and a small command schema. Part of that distance was measured, and most of it
was not.

\paragraph{The deployed system.} The acoustic-robustness experiment of the \emph{M\'emoire
d'Ing\'enieur}, which owns its design and analysis, ran the selected configuration on the board over
the golden set, and it supplies two facts this document needs. First, with reference text as input,
the parser's output on the board is identical to the workstation's on all 200 items, so the
\gls{em} figures of record, measured on the workstation, describe the deployed parser rather than a
proxy for it. The identity is shown for Qwen2.5-0.5B at Q4\_K\_M under one \texttt{llama.cpp} build,
not for the other configurations. Second, on the same items with speech as input, the deployed
system's \gls{crr} is 0.690 on clean audio and 0.590 at 10~dB \gls{snr}, against an \gls{em} of
0.935 on reference text. The accuracy the deployed system loses is therefore lost at the speech
stage, not at the parser. The \gls{em} figures of this document are best read as the parser's
accuracy given a correct transcript, a level the deployed system did not reach, and not as an
estimate of the system's accuracy on speech.

\paragraph{Other speakers.} The golden set is one speaker in one recording session. The parser
reads text and does not see the speaker, so the speaker enters only through the transcripts that
speech recognition produces. The speaker-sensitivity experiment places the author's \gls{wer}, 23.1\%,
near the median of a multi-speaker distribution (Section~\ref{sec:speaker-sensitivity}), which
suggests that the author is not an unusually easy speaker for the recogniser. The same experiment
reports a spread from 6.2\% to 49.5\% across accent groups, so for some speakers the parser's input
would be considerably worse than the author's, and nothing measured here says how the parser's
accuracy degrades on such input. Transfer to other speakers is therefore not established, and the
single session leaves the day-to-day variation of the author's own voice unmeasured as well.

\paragraph{Other hardware and runtimes.} Every timing figure comes from one Raspberry~Pi~5 with an
active cooler and one \texttt{llama.cpp} build. Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff} argued that the latency
axis depends on the board's thermal state, and a benchmark of language-model inference on \glspl{sbc} reports
throughput differences of up to four times between two runtimes on the same board~\cite{sbc2025}.
The latency coordinates, and the frontier they define, may therefore not transfer to another board,
another cooling arrangement or another runtime, and the margins of Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff} are
small enough that a modest change could reverse a comparison against the latency budget. Accuracy is
more portable, since it is a property of the weights and the decoder, but only where the runtime
tokenises input as training did. The tokeniser mismatch that kept H2O-Danube3-500M out of the
deployed comparison indicates that this portability has to be checked for each conversion rather
than assumed.

\paragraph{A small schema.} The task is single-turn, with ten intents, a bounded slot vocabulary and
in-domain test phrasings drawn from 12 command patterns. Two readings of
Chapter~\ref{chap:results} depend on that size. The first is that the grammar cost nothing
measurable in accuracy. The literature reports that constrained decoding can distort a model's
distribution over outputs~\cite{park2024} and degrade multi-step reasoning while matching or
improving classification~\cite{tam2024}; a task this close to classification sits where that cost
is least expected, so its absence here does not predict its absence on a larger schema or on a task
that requires intermediate reasoning. The second is that the fine-tune left the grammar almost
nothing to correct, one malformed output in 3{,}540 decodes. On a schema with more intents, nested
structure or longer outputs, the fine-tune may be less complete and the grammar's measurable
contribution larger. That possibility was not tested, and it is the setting in which the grammar's
reachability property would matter most.

\paragraph{Models and quantisation levels.} The results concern four fine-tuned models from four
families, three of them quantised at two levels, Q8\_0 and Q4\_K\_M. As
Section~\ref{sec:tradeoff} argued for the frontier, they are statements about these models and not
about parameter classes. The quantisation result, no significant accuracy difference between the two
levels within any model, likewise describes these two schemes under this recipe; lower bit widths
and other quantisation methods were not measured.
