\chapter{Demonstration, limitations and conclusion}
\label{chap:demonstration}
This chapter closes the document. Section~\ref{sec:demonstration-protocol} fixes the protocol of
the live demonstration planned for the defence, before it is run, and reports no result from it.
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what the measurements of Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} do not
establish, and Section~\ref{sec:future-work} the work that follows from each limitation.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} answers RQ2 and RQ3 against those measurements. Every figure in this
chapter is quoted from Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} and every verdict from
Table~\ref{tab:requirements-summary}; the chapter adds no measurement.

\section{Demonstration protocol}
\label{sec:demonstration-protocol}
% Author's decisions (24 Sep): two parts -- A on the kinematic backend (clamp active, 3D display
% of its state, the run of record, the only part judged), B on PyFlyt (rotor dynamics, no clamp,
% no criterion); no live-capture latency reported; the scaffold's script and four criteria.
The live-demonstration requirement of Table~\ref{tab:functional-requirements} asks for the full
pipeline to run end to end from a live microphone, with networking disabled on camera. The
demonstration is planned for the defence. Its protocol is fixed here, before the run, so that what
counts as success is decided before the outcome is known and cannot be adjusted to it. It is one
run and not an experiment: it can establish that the chain operates end to end, but it measures no
rate and no percentile, and Table~\ref{tab:requirements-summary} records the requirement as planned
rather than met.

\paragraph{What the run adds.} The demonstration is the only run that joins the parts
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} evaluates separately. The speech experiments reported there replay
recorded audio and end on the board, the latency experiment at the state machine and the
acoustic-robustness experiment at the validator, and the formation-control experiment drives the
controller without speech. The demonstration is the first run in which live capture feeds the
pipeline, in which a spoken command travels through the state machine to the swarm controller and
the simulated vehicles, and in which the wireless link from the device to the workstation carries a
command (Section~\ref{sec:command-bus}). It is also the end-to-end run with networking disabled on
which the offline-speech-recognition and offline-operation criteria are verified; until it is made,
both remain not yet run.

\paragraph{Setup.} The Raspberry~Pi~5 runs both paths, with the thread allocation of
Table~\ref{tab:core-allocation}, from the microphone through which the golden set was recorded. The
workstation runs the consumer of the bus, the flight state machine, the swarm controller and the
simulation, as Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} places them, and the two machines are joined by their
wireless link. At the start of the run the network interfaces of both machines are displayed on
camera: every interface is disabled except that link, which is the meaning
Section~\ref{sec:operational-context} gives to networking disabled. The video records the whole run
with a visible clock, and the state machine's log, which records every rejection with the intent
rejected and the state it arrived in (Section~\ref{sec:state-machine}), is kept beside it. The clock
orders events; it does not time them. No latency is reported from the run, because a single run
yields no percentile, and every latency figure in this document remains the one measured in
Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment}.

\paragraph{Two parts.} The run has two parts. Both use the same device, the same state machine and
the same controller with the same gains, and they differ only in the simulation backend behind the
environment interface (Section~\ref{sec:simulation-backends}). Part~A is the run of record. It flies
on the kinematic backend, the one from which every result of Section~\ref{sec:formation-control}
comes and the only one on which the separation clamp acts, and it follows the whole script of
Table~\ref{tab:demo-script}. Its vehicles are drawn in three dimensions at the positions the
kinematic backend computes, and the display adds no dynamics of its own. Part~B follows, with the
workstation's process restarted on the physics backend, and repeats steps~1, 2, 5 and~6 of the
script: the take-off, the circle, the line and the landing. Its vehicles have rigid-body and rotor
dynamics, and no separation clamp acts on them. Part~B puts the controller of
Section~\ref{sec:swarm-controller}, unretuned, under rigid-body dynamics, which otherwise only the
physics backend's tests do, and its outcome is not anticipated here. It carries no success
criterion. Separation in Part~B is
recorded rather than judged, and a close approach there falls under the limitation of
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, not under the verdict. Omitting Part~B, if the defence leaves no time
for it, withdraws no claim.

\paragraph{Success criteria.} Four criteria are fixed for Part~A, and the live-demonstration
requirement is met only if all four hold. First, every step of Table~\ref{tab:demo-script} ends in
the state and the behaviour its row gives. Second, at step~4 the hold takes effect and the command
spoken before it does not. The hold may meet that command during its decode, which it then cancels,
or earlier, during transcription, in which case the ordering rule discards the command when it is
published (Section~\ref{sec:command-bus}); in either case no line formation begins. Third, no
utterance that the validator or the state machine rejects, and none that resolves to
\texttt{unknown}, produces motion: in flight a rejection becomes a hold, and otherwise nothing is
dispatched (Section~\ref{sec:validation-layers}). Fourth, the whole run is on video with its clock,
from the display of the network interfaces to the reset. A criterion that fails is reported as
failed, together with the step at which it failed.

\paragraph{Scope of a single run.} One run, by one speaker in one room, establishes that the
chain operates, not how often it does. No rate, percentile or interval follows from it, and every
criterion of Table~\ref{tab:requirements-summary} that is judged on a rate keeps the verdict
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} gives it. The vehicles are simulated in both parts, and neither part
flies an airframe or models wind.

\paragraph{Prerequisites.} Three components the run requires are not built, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} records each where it would sit: live microphone capture
(Section~\ref{sec:audio-chain}), an entry point that starts the runtime as one process
(Section~\ref{sec:runtime}), and the link from the state machine to the swarm controller
(Section~\ref{sec:state-machine}). The link is more than a connection. The controller accepts only
a formation (Section~\ref{sec:swarm-controller}), while the script also commands a take-off, a
movement, a hold, a landing and an abort, so each of these needs a mapping onto the controller.
In addition, the state machine's transitions at the take-off height and on ground contact, like the reset,
require the vehicles' state reported back to it. Part~A requires, in addition, the three-dimensional display
of the kinematic backend. None of the four alters a stage measured in
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}: a capture source replaces the replay without a change to any stage
that follows it (Section~\ref{sec:audio-chain}), and the link and the display add to the chain
without changing the controller or any stage the experiments measured.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The findings reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} are bounded by the empirical and experimental
conditions under which they were measured. This section states what those measurements do not establish,
following the order of the architecture from simulation to speech. Every figure is inherited from
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}.

\paragraph{Kinematic simulation scope.} The formation-control results of
Section~\ref{sec:formation-control} come from the kinematic simulator alone. The kinematic backend
(\texttt{swarm/simulate.py}) models vehicles as ideal point masses responding to commanded
accelerations under semi-implicit double integration (Section~\ref{sec:simulation-backends}). It
omits rotor dynamics, aerodynamic downwash, ground effect, battery voltage discharge, and motor
saturation. Furthermore, it incorporates no environmental disturbances such as ambient wind or gust
perturbations. The convergence times (median 4.06~s for the circle, 3.26~s for the line, and 2.30~s for
the wedge) and trajectory profiles therefore evaluate the controller -- the formation law, the
potential field and the flocking terms -- under idealised kinematics rather than the flight envelope
of physical aircraft.

\paragraph{Transfer to rigid-body dynamics.} Transfer of the swarm controller to rigid-body physics is
not established (Section~\ref{sec:simulation-backends}). The physics backend, constructed on
PyFlyt~\cite{pyflyt}, simulates rigid-body quadrotor dynamics with rotor physics stepping at 240~Hz.
Because the underlying \texttt{QuadX} attitude controller steps twice per invocation, each control tick
advances 10 physics steps (41.67~ms), yielding an effective control rate of 24~Hz rather than the
nominal 48~Hz or 50~Hz (Section~\ref{sec:simulation-backends}). The 600 ticks evaluate 25~s of simulated
flight with the controller operating at half its intended frequency. Moreover, the physics backend is
exercised only by unit and smoke tests across a single initial placement (seed~42), each for 600 ticks: 12~s at the nominal 20~ms tick and
25~s of simulated flight at the effective one. These tests
verify only that the terminal positions of the five vehicles lie within 0.5~m of their assigned slots;
they record no time-resolved trajectories, apply no separation clamp, and count no collisions. Whether
the controller maintains safe separation and stable flight under rigid-body dynamics across varied
placements remains unmeasured.

\paragraph{Integrator separation clamp.} The collision-free flight observed in
Section~\ref{sec:formation-control} rests directly on the geometric separation clamp. Across all 150
trials, zero collisions were observed, backed by a hard geometric separation clamp at the integrator.
On the kinematic backend, this clamp operates by directly rewriting vehicle position states at the
integrator whenever inter-vehicle distance drops below 0.800~m (Section~\ref{sec:swarm-controller}).
Collisions are evaluated after the clamp against a threshold of 0.35~m, below the clamp distance, so zero collisions follows from
the numerical convergence of the clamp. The clamp intervened 432 times across 144 of the 150 trials,
requiring between 1 and 14 corrections in 96\% of runs. A physical quadrotor airframe or a rigid-body
physics simulator exposes no internal position state to rewrite; collision avoidance on physical
vehicles must act through commanded accelerations, attitude setpoints, or rotor thrust. The clamp
mechanism therefore cannot be transferred to physical airframes without reformulation.

\paragraph{Velocity ceiling during formation changes.} The velocity ceiling is not enforced where motion
occurs (Section~\ref{sec:swarm-controller}). The semantic validator bounds commanded velocities at
2.0~m/s for \texttt{move} and \texttt{set\_param} commands (Section~\ref{sec:validation-layers}), but
formation commands carry no speed parameter. Both simulation backends enforce a hard ceiling of 3.0~m/s
on vehicle velocity. A formation change can therefore accelerate the vehicles up to the backend limit of 3.0~m/s, above the
validator's 2.0~m/s boundary; vehicle speeds were not recorded in the formation-control experiment, so how
often this occurs is not measured. Because the geometric separation clamp bounds inter-vehicle distance
rather than speed, nothing in the controller keeps formation manoeuvres within the commanded flight
envelope.

\paragraph{Acoustic scope and recorded speech.} The acoustic evaluation relies entirely on recorded
audio replayed through the runtime. Live microphone capture introduces analog gain variations, room
reverberation, ambient noise, and capture buffer latency, none of which is present in the replayed
stream. Furthermore, the 200 golden-set recordings of Section~\ref{sec:acoustic-robustness} represent a
single speaker in a single recording session. Cross-speaker variance, pitch variation, and regional
accents are not evaluated in this document; speaker sensitivity across diverse speakers and accent groups
is evaluated in the \emph{M\'emoire de Master}.

\paragraph{Loopback transport and wireless link.} The pipeline was evaluated in decoupled stages rather
than across a physical wireless network. In the latency experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment}), the command bus, consumer, and flight state machine executed
locally on the Raspberry~Pi~5 over its loopback interface. The physical wireless (Wi-Fi) link between the
Raspberry~Pi~5 and the workstation never carried a command. Transmission latency, packet jitter, and
radio contention are therefore excluded from the measured end-to-end latency figure (3{,}122~ms at p95).
Furthermore, commands on the bus are transmitted as unacknowledged \gls{udp} datagrams; while zero packet
loss occurred over loopback, an unacknowledged \gls{udp} datagram transmitted over physical Wi-Fi is
vulnerable to packet loss without transport-layer retransmission.

\paragraph{Preemption queueing and cancellation boundaries.} Preemption covers only the utterance
currently in progress (Section~\ref{sec:dual-path}). An utterance endpointed and queued behind an active
decode reserves its sequence number only upon dequeue. Consequently, following a spoken \texttt{swarm hold},
a command already waiting in the queue receives a higher sequence number and is dispatched while the swarm
is in \texttt{FLYING}. (After \texttt{swarm abort}, the state machine rejects it in \texttt{ABORTED}.)
In addition, the cancellation mechanism reaches only an active language-model decode. If a reflex
trigger arrives while an utterance is undergoing \gls{stt} transcription, transcription and the
subsequent decode proceed to completion before the ordering rule discards the result. In that event,
recovery latency is governed by the remaining parse-path stages rather than by the cancellation. The
preemption-recovery criterion was measured only for triggers arriving during a decode, and it is missed
there as well (1{,}195~ms at p95 against 300~ms).

\paragraph{Safe-failure rate.} The safe-failure rate fails the $\geq 0.70$ requirement at every noise
level. In clean audio, the safe-failure rate is 0.032 (2 of 62 failures), and it remains between 0.014 and
0.039 from 20~dB down to 5~dB \gls{snr} (Section~\ref{sec:acoustic-robustness}). As noise increases and
total failures rise from 62 to 103, safe failures remain between one and four: acoustic degradation does
not steer corrupted utterances toward the safe exit of \texttt{unknown} or \texttt{hover}, but emits
executable, well-formed commands with altered slots. On reference text, the golden set yields 13
failures and zero safe failures. Speech recognition errors therefore do not make failures less safe than
the parser's own; neither input approaches the 0.70 bound.

\paragraph{Uncaught error classes and state machine fallback.} Two error classes bypass all validation
layers (Section~\ref{sec:safety-problem}). First, parameter values outside the physical envelope are
clamped to boundary limits and dispatched rather than rejected. Second, syntactically valid,
in-envelope, and state-legal commands that misinterpret the operator's intention are executed without
detection. In addition, the state machine exhibits an internal defect:
\texttt{FlightStateMachine.handle\_command} evaluates state legality on the command's original intent
before the validator's fallback replacement (Section~\ref{sec:state-machine}). While unreachable on the
deployed route (where validation occurs before publication), an unvalidated command such as
\texttt{set\_param} without a parameter, submitted during landing, is replaced with a hold and
dispatched, despite holds being illegal during landing.

\paragraph{Summary of criteria verdicts.} Table~\ref{tab:requirements-summary} records seven criteria
met, five missed, two not demonstrated, two not yet run, and one planned for the defence. Each of the
five misses has a confirmed cause. Reflex latency (545~ms idle, 547~ms loaded at p95 against 150~ms) is
missed in 540~ms that combine the spotter's decision delay, the 80~ms frame quantisation and the error of
the energy-based offset anchor, which the measurement does not separate, and not in board computation
(6~ms idle, 18~ms loaded). End-to-end latency (3{,}122~ms at p95 against 2{,}500~ms, missed by 622~ms or
25\%) is missed in speech recognition (1{,}449~ms against 1{,}200~ms allowance) and prompt prefill
(751~ms against 250~ms allowance, operating without prompt prefix caching). Preemption recovery
(1{,}195~ms at p95 against 300~ms) is missed in the time \texttt{llama-server} takes to
release its slot, which its 1~s HTTP polling interval explains only in part
(Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment}). Clean-audio recognition (\gls{crr} 0.690 against 0.80) is missed in speech recognition
errors and 22 failures from endpointer pauses. Recognition in noise (\gls{crr} 0.590 at 10~dB against
0.65) is missed in acoustic degradation under noise. The two keyword criteria are not demonstrated under
the interval rule: keyword false accepts (2.33/h [0.06, 12.98] against $\leq 1$/h) has a point estimate
above the budget, but its interval spans the threshold; keyword false rejects (hold 0.050 [0.001, 0.249],
abort 0.000 [0.000, 0.168] against $\leq 0.10$) have point estimates within budget, but intervals
exceeding 0.10 due to the 20 takes per class. Offline speech recognition and offline operation remain not
yet run because neither benchmark evaluated the system with networking disabled on camera. The
single-command rerun that reproducibility requires (Section~\ref{sec:engineering-requirements}) is not
provided either: each experiment is rerun by its own runner (Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}).

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:future-work}
Seven engineering and experimental developments follow directly from the limitations identified in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Each item addresses a specific empirical or architectural gap.

\paragraph{Live acoustic pipeline and physical wireless deployment.} Following the limitation of
recorded audio replay and loopback transport, the first priority is integrating live microphone capture
on the Raspberry~Pi~5 and deploying the command bus over a physical Wi-Fi connection to the workstation.
This development requires measuring analog input buffering, ambient acoustic noise, radio packet
jitter, and transmission latency under operational conditions. Furthermore, because stop commands on the
bus are transmitted as unacknowledged \gls{udp} datagrams, transport reliability under packet loss must
be evaluated, or hardened with redundant transmission. Executing the demonstration protocol of
Table~\ref{tab:demo-script} repeatedly over physical wireless links will establish empirical latency and
reliability distributions across multiple trials.

\paragraph{Velocity and thrust-level collision avoidance.} Following the limitation of integrator
position rewriting, the geometric separation clamp must be replaced with an active collision avoidance
mechanism compatible with physical quadrotors and rigid-body simulators. In physical flight, position
states cannot be overwritten. Collision avoidance must be formulated at the acceleration, velocity, or
rotor thrust level with kinematic feasibility constraints, since the potential field already
present is a soft mechanism whose failure modes are known~\cite{koren1991}. Such a formulation must
explicitly enforce the 2.0~m/s speed ceiling during formation reconfigurations, bounding vehicle
velocities within the safety envelope.

\paragraph{Rigid-body simulation and transfer to airframes.} Following the limitation that transfer to
rigid-body dynamics is unestablished, the swarm controller must be comprehensively evaluated under
rigid-body physics across diverse initial configurations. PyFlyt's control tick interface must be
aligned with the controller's nominal 50~Hz rate, eliminating the 24~Hz stepping discrepancy
(Section~\ref{sec:simulation-backends}). Following validation in rigid-body simulation with simulated
aerodynamic disturbances and rotor downwash, the control pipeline should be transferred to physical
quadrotor airframes equipped with onboard attitude stabilisation.

\paragraph{Targeted latency reduction.} Following the
end-to-end latency shortfall (3{,}122~ms against 2{,}500~ms) and preemption recovery shortfall
(1{,}195~ms against 300~ms), engineering effort must focus on the two stages that exceed their budget
allowances (Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment}). Enabling prompt prefix caching in the
language-model server is expected to reduce prefill latency from 751~ms towards its 250~ms allowance,
provided the cached prefix leaves the decoded command unchanged, which was not tested
(Section~\ref{sec:runtime}). For speech recognition, which accounts for 1{,}449~ms at p95, deploying an
optimised acoustic model or streaming encoder is expected to compress transcription time toward the
1{,}200~ms budget. Replacing \texttt{llama-server}'s 1~s HTTP polling interval with an explicit slot
cancellation callback is expected to shorten preemption recovery, although the polling period does not
account for all of it: the p95 of 1{,}195~ms and the maximum of 1{,}505~ms both exceed the 1~s period,
and that remainder is unexplained (Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment}).

\paragraph{Recognition accuracy.} Following the clean-audio and noise recognition misses, which lie in
the speech stage (Section~\ref{sec:acoustic-robustness}), the remedies deferred in
Chapter~\ref{chap:validation} can be evaluated: a longer endpointing silence threshold, which would
reduce the splits at pauses at a cost to the endpointing wait; a larger recognition model, at a cost to
the 1{,}200~ms recognition allowance; and a different initial prompt. None of them may be chosen on the
golden set, which is the test set. Each requires a second held-out set of recorded speech on which it is
selected before the golden set judges it.

\paragraph{Preemption queue flushing and transcription interruption.} Following the limitations in
preemption scope, the runtime preemption mechanism must be extended beyond active decodes. The command
bus consumer must be modified to flush pending utterances from the input queue upon receiving a reflex
trigger, preventing queued commands from dispatching after a hold. In addition, preemption signals must
be propagated directly into the \gls{stt} engine to abort in-progress transcription immediately upon
a reflex trigger, bounding recovery latency when an abort or hold arrives during speech recognition.

\paragraph{Confidence gating and state machine validation alignment.} Following the safe-failure rate
deficiency (0.032 against $\geq 0.70$) and the state machine raw-intent defect, two software corrections
are required. To direct acoustic errors toward the safe exit, a confidence gate based on decoder token
log-probabilities should be introduced, routing utterances with low confidence to \texttt{unknown} rather
than dispatching executable commands. In the flight state machine,
\texttt{FlightStateMachine.handle\_command} must be corrected to evaluate state legality strictly on the
post-validation command, ensuring that invalid commands replaced with holds cannot be dispatched in
states where holds are illegal.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This chapter concludes the \emph{M\'emoire d'Ing\'enieur}. The system was designed, built, and evaluated
to answer two research questions on pipeline latency, acoustic robustness, and control quality. Those
questions are answered directly against the measured data of Chapter~\ref{chap:validation}.

\paragraph{Pipeline latency.} Research Question~2 (RQ2) asks whether a fully offline pipeline
(keyword spotter, \gls{vad}, \gls{stt}, grammar-constrained \gls{slm}, validator, and controller) can
meet a p95 budget compatible with interactive swarm control, and how that budget is distributed across
stages.

The measured data answer the first clause in the negative: the deployed pipeline, whose models all run on the
Raspberry~Pi~5, does not meet the 2{,}500~ms end-to-end p95 budget for interactive swarm control. Two scope
conditions attach to that answer. Offline operation itself was not verified, since no run was made with
networking disabled, and the measured spans end when the command is handed to the command bus on the
device, so the wireless hop and the controller lie outside them. Measured from the end of speech
across 226 golden-set segments on the cooled Raspberry~Pi~5, the parse path reaches an end-to-end p95 of
3{,}122~ms (3{,}602~ms with endpointing), missing the requirement by 622~ms (25\%). Similarly, the reflex
path does not meet its 150~ms p95 budget, reaching 545~ms idle and 547~ms loaded from the keyword offset,
and preemption recovery reaches 1{,}195~ms at p95 against its 300~ms budget.

The second clause is answered by the per-stage latency attribution of Table~\ref{tab:latency-budget},
which accounts for every stage of both paths. On the parse path, three stages meet their
allowances: endpointing silence (480~ms against 500~ms), \gls{slm} decode (915~ms against 1{,}100~ms),
and validation with the state-machine check (under 1~ms against 50~ms). The shortfall lies in two
stages: speech recognition, which takes 1{,}449~ms against its 1{,}200~ms allowance, and prompt
prefill, which takes 751~ms against its 250~ms allowance because the deployed runtime does not cache the
prompt prefix. On the reflex path, board computation and publication require 6~ms idle and 18~ms loaded;
the remaining 540~ms combines the spotter's decision delay, the 80~ms frame quantisation and the error
of the offset anchor, which the measurement does not separate.

\paragraph{Acoustic robustness and control quality.} Research Question~3 (RQ3) asks how
\gls{crr} and \gls{wer} degrade as \gls{snr} falls from clean to 5~dB under propeller noise, whether the
system degrades safely rather than wrongly, and whether the controller holds formation to specification.

The first clause is answered by the acoustic-robustness evaluation across five noise conditions.
Recognition degrades substantially as noise increases: \gls{crr} falls from 0.690 [0.625, 0.750] in
clean audio to 0.650 at 15~dB, 0.590 [0.520, 0.660] at 10~dB, and 0.485 at 5~dB, while \gls{wer} rises
from 23.3\% clean to 28.8\% at 10~dB and 36.0\% at 5~dB. Both clean recognition ($\geq 0.80$) and
recognition in noise ($\geq 0.65$ at 10~dB) miss their requirements. The speech recognition stage
accounts for the entire accuracy loss: relative to the parser's reference-text \gls{em} of 0.935, the
speech stage costs 0.245 of accuracy in clean audio, 0.345 at 10~dB, and 0.450 at 5~dB.

The second clause is answered in the negative: the system does not degrade safely. The safe-failure rate
is 0.032 in clean audio (2 of 62 failures) and remains between 0.014 and 0.039 at every noise level,
failing the $\geq 0.70$ requirement. When acoustic noise induces a recognition failure, the pipeline
dispatches an executable command with altered parameters rather than routing the utterance to
\texttt{unknown} or \texttt{hover}.

The third clause is answered in the affirmative for kinematic simulation: the controller holds formation
to specification. Across all 150 trials on the kinematic backend, zero collisions were observed, backed
by a hard geometric separation clamp at the integrator that intervened 432 times across 144 trials.
Formation accuracy reached 1.000 in every trial, exceeding the $\geq 0.85$ requirement. The measure is
saturated by the protocol, since every trial converged within 4.32~s of a 60~s trial, so it does not
discriminate between the formations; convergence time is the figure that does
(Section~\ref{sec:formation-control}).

\paragraph{System contribution.} The contribution this document claims
(Section~\ref{sec:contributions}) is the membership rule of the reflex path, derived from the cost of a
false accept and the value of the time saved rather than chosen by preference, together with the latency
budget of its own against which the path is specified and measured while the language model decodes. The rule admits exactly two
classes, whose false accepts move the swarm towards a safer state at a cost to the mission rather than to
safety, and it states the condition a third class would have to meet to be admitted, which is what makes
it auditable. Running a fast path beside the language model is not itself claimed
(Section~\ref{sec:research-gaps}). In the latency experiment the reflex path cancelled 78 of 78 in-progress
decodes, the command bus never had to discard a late result, and neither class triggered on the other's
phrase; its latency nevertheless misses the 150~ms budget (Section~\ref{sec:latency-experiment}).
Measured from the onset of the keyword, the reflex takes 965~ms at the median (Table~\ref{tab:latency-budget}),
longer than the 466~ms that MIRA's gate reports from speech onset (Section~\ref{sec:soa-dual-path}).
What the reflex adds over that gate is therefore not speed but a trigger restricted to two commands by a derived rule,
and a budget against which its latency is located; that budget is missed in the spotter's detection, not on the board.
